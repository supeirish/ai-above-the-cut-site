The week in three numbers
$16.8BAnthropic's revenue for July to September, about 45% above the quarter before, the FT reported. Four quarters at that level would be about $67 billion. OpenAI's yearly pace was about $50 billion in early October, roughly double its $25 billion at the end of February.
Why it matters: both leading labs are growing fast, and Anthropic's scale is approaching OpenAI's. The figures aren't directly comparable: Anthropic counts sales made through cloud partners, which OpenAI reports differently. Investors are weighing that growth against huge spending commitments. (Simon Willison on the FT report · CNBC · Bloomberg on OpenAI, Oct 8 · RTÉ on OpenAI, Mar 5 · Axios on the accounting, Oct 8)
2xHow much higher Google's Gemini 4 Argon scored on a well-known reasoning test in a setup like Google's own (97.5%) than in a generic setup every model gets (49.8%). OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra showed a similar gap in September: 99.9% vs 54.8%.
Why it matters: two leading models now show the same pattern. Google's shared-setup run completed only 42 of 55 tasks, so the gap isn't a clean measure of the setup's effect. But the point for buyers holds: a single published score won't tell you how a model will perform in your own setup. (ARC Prize: Argon · ARC Prize: Astra)

Why it matters: Gartner says owners would need $8.5 trillion of revenue through 2030 to cover their costs and a 12% return, and it expects no more than $3.6 trillion. (Gartner, Oct 14)

01 · The One Thing
Google released Gemini 4 Argon, its most capable AI model, on October 13, to developers, paying subscribers and businesses at once. It is available in Google's developer tools and Google Cloud, in the Gemini app and Search for paying subscribers, and in Gemini for business.

The introductory price is $2 per million tokens in and $10 out (a token is a piece of a word) until November 30. Then it doubles. Google says Argon ranks first on the Vals Index, a test of finance, legal, tax and coding work. (Google, Oct 13 · Google pricing)

  The executive shift: Google is back among the top model makers, and the leaders are close. On Epoch AI's index, which combines many tests, Argon scored 165, against 166 for OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra and 167 for Anthropic's Opus 5.5, The Decoder reported. Yet on one reasoning test, Argon scored 97.5% in one setup and 49.8% in another. For a buyer, the question is not how close the published scores are. It is how the model performs where your work gets done. Test it in your own setup, at the December price. (The Decoder · ARC Prize)
02Do This Week
1 · Test the model and its setup as one system, and change one at a time. Owner: whoever runs your AI platform. Our read: when you switch models, keep your tools and instructions fixed and rerun real tasks. When you change the tools, keep the model fixed. Stakes: Argon scored about twice as high in Google's setup as in a generic one, and almost half of the bigger paying teams on one developer gateway changed their main model in a single month (Section 06).
2 · Pick one process where AI output is piling up, and redesign the step it waits on. Owner: the executive who owns that process. Our read: ask what the waiting step is for, and whether it can change shape rather than just get more staff. Anthropic's reviewers now check what each change is meant to do instead of reading every line. Stakes: last week's study of 718 companies found more code, slower review and no measurable rise in finished work (The Synthesis).
3 · List the AI features that switched on inside your work tools this month. Owner: your chief information officer. Our read: check each against your data and records rules. Stakes: Gmail's new AI Inbox arrived with "no admin control" (The Signal).
03The Signal

This week's question: as AI spreads into more of the work, who is checking it, and who is paying for it?

Trust  Scrutiny of AI firms grew in three forms: lawsuits, investigations and a White House charter. None adds a new rule yet. Primary: state attorney general filings. Reported: NBC, Axios, The Next Web.

Alleged in court. Eight states sued OpenAI on October 14 under their consumer-protection laws, alleging it misled people about ChatGPT's safety, including for children. Arkansas asks a court to stop "further advanced AI testing" until safeguards exist. OpenAI says it has built "industry-leading age-specific protections."

Under investigation. The FTC sent OpenAI and Anthropic demands for documents, Axios reported. The EU's AI Office sent requests about security and outside testing.

New rules: none yet. The White House AI task force's charter says labs are "ultimately responsible" for their models' risks, but defines no incident, reporting deadline or penalty.

Our read: the states aren't waiting for AI rules. They are using consumer-protection laws that already apply. (NBC DFW · Iowa AG · Arkansas AG · Axios · Euronews · European Commission · The Next Web)

Horizon  Lenders and investors started putting a price on AI risk. Reported: Reuters, Axios, CNN. After Anthropic's big quarter, some investors discussed valuing it at about $1.5 trillion in its planned stock sale, below the roughly $2 trillion expected, Reuters reported. Nothing is agreed. CoreWeave, which rents out AI computing power, fell more than 15% on October 14, CNN reported, and its bond yield hit a record high, Axios reported. South Korea's main stock index fell 7.6% on October 15, led by memory-chip makers. Our read: investors now want evidence that AI revenue will cover the bill. (Reuters, via WMBD · Axios · CNN))
Work  AI is switching on inside the tools people already use, sometimes without an off switch for IT. Primary: Google. Reported: Android Authority. Gmail's new AI Inbox, which pulls to-dos and summaries out of email, began reaching paid Workspace accounts on October 12. Google's page says: "There is no admin control for this feature." Gmail Live, which answers spoken questions about your inbox, is on by default but follows existing IT settings. Our read: the vendor now sets the default. A company's AI policy has to cover what arrives switched on. (Google: AI Inbox · Android Authority: Gmail Live)
Deploy  Big banks blocked outside AI agents from logging in to customer accounts. Reported: Fortune, American Banker, FT. JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon said the bank is "not going to allow just any outside agent" inside. "You don't want rogue agents." Schwab said it blocks agents that use clients' passwords, and points clients to its own assistant, Charley. The FT reported Citi is restricting agents too. Both JPMorgan and Schwab still allow some approved routes; what they shut is the side door. Our read: agents that act wherever you're signed in, like ChatGPT's new feature, now meet businesses that decide which agents get in. Any company with customer logins will need that rule. (Fortune · American Banker · FT)
Proof  A grocer plans corporate job cuts, and a software vendor went after the money companies spend on people. Reported: AP, The Register. Kroger plans to cut 3,000 corporate jobs over three years. Its memo cites technology, not AI; AP tied the move to Kroger's AI plans. Workday told investors its agents could reach "beyond the software budgets into the labor and services spend," billed by use. Our read: Workday wants a share of what companies spend on staff and outside services, not just software. The question for buyers is who keeps the savings. (AP, via ABC News · The Register)
Below the Cut

05The Synthesis
When the work outgrows the system that manages it

Last week we looked at a study of 718 companies. After they adopted AI coding agents, code sent for review rose 23% per worker, each review took 49% longer, and finished work did not measurably rise (Issue 017). The work had piled up at the step after the code was written.

This week Anthropic described a related strain at a different step. Its problem was less the people reviewing code than the system that tracks and merges it. In May it merged about 2,500 code changes a day. By September it was merging more than 30,000, about twelve times as many. Git, the standard tool developers use to track and merge changes, could no longer keep up. Anthropic says Git's design, built for people working offline on full copies of the code, was "actively undermining" its ability to grow once most of the work came from always-online AI agents. So it replaced Git for its main code base. Primary source. (Anthropic, Oct 13)

What changed was the process, not just the volume.

A small team. Seven engineers built the new system, called Scion, and a new code-review tool, and moved the main code base over in five months.
AI built the tools that manage AI's output. Claude helped write both. When Claude rewrote an early prototype of one part of the system, the new version used 20 times less memory and ran 4 times faster. Every engineer on the project would now rate Claude "better at writing code than them," Anthropic writes.
People did the judging. Anthropic writes that the engineers' role "was to tell Claude 'no.'" They steered it away from over-engineering. Its adversarial review is effective, it says, but "can also be myopic." Engineers now review the intent of each change rather than reading every line.
The result, by Anthropic's count. Scion uses 200 times less processing power and memory than the fleet of Git copies it replaced, and automated testing gained 25% in processing capacity.

What we do not know. Anthropic gives no defect or rollback rate, so there is no way to tell whether quality held at twelve times the volume. Every number is Anthropic's, about its own work. And Anthropic says Scion "is by no means a suitable replacement for git for general use." It makes sense only at very large scale. GitHub, Microsoft's code-hosting service, said in April that "agentic development workflows have accelerated sharply," so the pressure is not unique to one lab. (GitHub, Apr 28)

A different choice, outside software. Britain's Home Office uses an AI tool built on OpenAI's GPT-4o to write summaries of asylum interviews. After it was rolled out in April, 45 of the 203 summaries that got an initial expert check failed it, or 22%. An earlier, separate pilot evaluation rejected 9%, so the two rates are not a clean before-and-after. The figures come from a report by the Open Rights Group, a digital-rights campaign, based on freedom-of-information requests. The group estimates experts checked only 0.3% of summaries from April to July. Applicants are not specifically told when AI was used and cannot correct the summaries. The government says people still make the decisions; the Home Office did not answer The Register's questions. Reported. (The Register, Oct 13) Unlike Anthropic, which redesigned its systems for faster production, the Home Office rolled out AI summaries with very little expert checking. The two cases are not the same kind of work. They show two different choices about how to redesign work once AI changes how it gets produced.

Analysis: Anthropic did not make yesterday's process faster. It asked which parts of that process were built around limits that no longer hold. Git assumes each developer works offline on a full copy of the code; Anthropic's agents are always online. A merge queue that locks the whole code base for each change "doesn't make much sense at agent scale." More of the testing now happens after code is merged. Reviewers now check what a change is meant to do instead of reading every line. Each of those habits made sense when people wrote code at human speed. Most companies are nowhere near Anthropic's scale. But Britain's asylum tool shows the risk of changing how work gets produced without equal care for how it gets checked. So the question for any process now carrying AI's output is: which of its steps exist because of a limit that is gone? The engineers did not become less important. Their job moved toward design, judgment and knowing when to say no.

06Where the Minds Disagree
 
Which score should a buyer trust: the maker's setup, or a shared one?

ARC-AGI-3 is a reasoning test of small puzzle games, run by the nonprofit ARC Prize. In its shared setup, every model gets the same plain interface and keeps only the notes it chooses to write down. In the provider setup, the model keeps its own private working state between turns, as it does in its maker's products. That wrapper of memory, tools and instructions is called a harness. At the same low setting, Argon scored 97.5% in the provider setup and 49.8% in the shared one, which finished only 42 of 55 games. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra showed a similar gap in September. (ARC Prize · ARC Prize, Sep 3)

The case for the maker's setup. You buy the system, not the bare model. If you will run Argon through Google's own tools, Google's setup is the one most likely to predict your results. Keeping private working state between turns is real engineering, and part of what you pay for.

The case for the shared setup. It is the only fair way to compare models. And a company that runs a model inside its own product uses its own setup, not the maker's. Teams are switching often: on Vercel's gateway, 48% of paying teams with steady use changed their most-used model in September. (Vercel)

Our read: use the shared score to compare models, but trust neither score to predict your own results. The provider score shows what Google's own setup achieved. The shared setup gives buyers a more consistent comparison, though this run was cut short. If the model will sit inside your own tools, your own test should decide. If you buy Google's complete product, test that product. And Vercel shows how often teams change models, not whether their results got better or worse. For a buyer, that missing number is the one that matters.
