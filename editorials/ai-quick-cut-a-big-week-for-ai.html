AI Quick Cut: A big week for AI. The payoff is still hard to see.

Three stories from the last few weeks of AI news, moving at three different speeds, and my quick read on what connects them.

By Mario Suarez · November 15, 2026 · 8 min read

This is an opinion piece. The sourced news brief for that week is Issue 022: AI agents want to shop for your customers. Who lets them in?.

Also published on LinkedIn, where you can join the discussion.

This is the first AI Quick Cut, a shorter post for when I have something to share between my longer pieces.

This week, while putting together my Sunday email for AI Above the Cut, I lined up the last few issues side by side. What struck me wasn't any one headline. It was that three stories are running at once, at very different speeds.

The money is unsettled

Anthropic is about to sell shares to the public for the first time. At the top of its price range, it would be valued at up to $1.4 trillion, counting stock options and other shares not yet issued, with trading expected November 19. Its filings show sales up about 47% in one quarter. They also show the cost of computing power eating 58% of its revenue, up from 48% the quarter before.

In the same week, the founder of Nvidia, the biggest maker of AI chips, stepped down as CEO, and the stock fell 4.3% that day. Nvidia had set out to raise $500 billion from outside investors to build AI data centers. So far, no loans have reportedly closed. Two weeks ago, Google's parent company lost $500 billion in value in a single day.

So which way is this going? This week, the answer was both. Everything is moving so fast that it's hard to see where the big bets could go wrong. And for the companies placing them, slowing down can feel like the bigger risk.

We'll learn more this week. Nvidia reports its quarterly results on Tuesday, its first since the founder stepped down. And Anthropic's shares are expected to start trading on Thursday.

The payoff is hard to see

Companies are spending a lot. One estimate, from Menlo Ventures, which invests in Anthropic, puts it at $177 billion a year, up from $33 billion in 2025. What they're getting back is harder to pin down.

  • 4%. Of the customer service leaders Gartner surveyed, 4% said they'd gotten the return they expected from AI.
  • 59%. In a Capgemini survey of 500 senior executives, 59% said their AI spending was significantly higher than they'd expected or budgeted. Only a third said they could confidently estimate the costs that don't show up on the bill, like the time people spend checking and fixing AI's work.
  • One of 21. Each week, I track 21 public companies for evidence linking AI to better margins. One, Duolingo, has made that link in a filing, citing lower costs for its own AI features.
  • 83%. It isn't all thin. Northern Trust says an AI agent, meaning AI that carries out steps on its own, cut the time to prepare a new client's data from two and a half weeks to three days, an 83% drop. That count includes the time people spent reviewing and checking the work. It's the bank's own number.

These numbers mostly measure hopes and costs. They don't say AI isn't working. They say the gains are uneven, and we're not yet good at knowing what we'll get, or what it will cost.

The products keep coming

Much of what launched this fall is built around agents: AI that doesn't just answer, but takes actions for you, like booking, buying, or sending. Over three weeks in September, Meta, Anthropic, Microsoft, and OpenAI each introduced or expanded an agent that keeps working after you close the laptop. Here is some of what has been announced or rolled out since Labor Day.

  • Sep 8. Meta launched Muse, a personal agent that handles email, travel, and shopping.
  • Sep 16. Anthropic combined Claude's chat with Cowork, which lets you hand over a whole job that keeps running after you close the laptop.
  • Sep 25. Microsoft announced Autopilot, an agent that keeps working while you're away.
  • Sep 29. OpenAI launched Dots, agents that you name, give goals to, and leave running. Meta opened Muse to small businesses.
  • Oct 8. Google tested a work agent that takes a goal and returns finished work.
  • Oct 13. Google released Gemini 4 Argon, its most capable AI yet. Workday added an agent that employees use for things like approving expenses.
  • Oct 21. Anthropic previewed Claude Duet, an agent that keeps going after you leave. Walmart began letting approved agents shop on its site.
  • Oct 29. Google's Gemini app began creating and editing documents, spreadsheets, and slides.
  • Nov 5. Claude began reading bank and card accounts, look but don't touch, in a US test.
  • Nov 12 and 13. Google put its newest Gemini into Search worldwide. OpenAI gave ChatGPT a wallet, so it can pay for things once you approve.
  • This week. Claude added spreadsheets, whiteboards, and meeting tools.

Google says its Gemini app has passed one billion monthly active users. OpenAI says 1.2 billion people use ChatGPT every week.

Reflections on recent news

Capability isn't the same as value. What AI can do isn't the same as what we get from it. We've bought a lot of capability. Together, we're still learning where to point it.

More and more people are finding real ways for AI to help in their work, me included. So the question isn't really whether it works. The harder question is why the gains are so uneven. Here are five things I see shaping this quest for value.

1. The bill is arriving before the proof. Across the last few weeks, the cost stories have had a similar shape: AI that is billed by use, capped, and running over budget. And part of the cost never shows up on the bill. It's the work of owning what AI produces: explaining what you want, then checking and fixing what comes back. I've been calling that the Delegation Tax, and companies pay it as much as individuals do. Most budgets have a line for people and a line for technology. AI sits somewhere in between. We pay for it like software, and we manage it more like a person. (More on that idea here.) Until budgets account for both, it will be hard to know what AI really costs.

2. Companies are drawing lines. Some of the biggest stories this month were about who gets to use AI, and for what. Goldman Sachs reportedly pulled AI coding tools from most of its bankers and traders. United and the largest US banks moved to keep out the AI agents that customers send to act for them. Some insurers are adding AI exclusions to standard liability policies. Each reflects a real concern about risk. The open question is what it costs. Limiting a tool is different from limiting who can find a better way to work, and the people closest to the work are often the first to see one. (More on that idea here.)

3. The products are ahead of how we use them. Many of the new agents are built to stay with you and keep working toward a goal. But most of what we ask AI to do is still a task: one piece of work, like pay for this or summarize that. Uber's CEO noted that booking a ride already takes about 50 seconds, so an agent has to do more than save a few taps. I'd argue the bigger opportunity is a mission: an outcome you give AI and keep managing over time. What makes a mission pay off is that the corrections stick. In my own work, when the same broken link turned up twice, the fix that mattered wasn't the link. It was moving the check earlier, so every later version arrived already checked.

The most useful correction is the one that changes every run after it.

4. Faster work still has to get through other people. AI can help one person finish an analysis faster. The people who act on it still need to understand it, question it, and agree on what to do. A working paper using data from 655 companies found that a year after adopting AI coding agents, code submissions per worker rose by about 36%. The average time from submitting code to getting it approved grew by about 56%. Finished software grew much less. AI can make more than people can take in. That may be why one person's gains can be real while a company's results stay hard to see.

5. Value for people isn't as prominent as I'd like. When I launched this newsletter, I wrote about two bridges we have to cross. The first runs from capability to value: turning what AI can do into something a business or a person actually gets from it. The second runs from value to human good: making sure that value helps people, and more people than before. Most of this fall's news has been about the first bridge. Much less of it has been about the second: what AI does for us in our own lives, and for each other. People are finding a voice, though. On Election Day, voters favored limits on data centers in 46 of 47 local ballot measures, by Ballotpedia's count. Those ranged from outright bans to requiring a public vote before a project could go ahead. It's hard to sort through business value and what AI means for our lives and our communities at the same time. But I think we have to keep both bridges in view, because neither one gets crossed by accident.

I'm following these questions in a series called "AI: Delegate, Collaborate, or Keep It Human." Part 1 looked at how one person gets good at working with AI. Part 2, coming next, is about judgment: what to hand over, how to check what comes back, and which decisions stay ours. Part 3 is about how teams decide together. I'm still working through all of this, and I'd like to learn from what you're seeing.

I'd love your answer in the comments: What evidence of AI paying off have you found most convincing?

Mario
