AI: Delegate, Collaborate, or Keep It Human, Part 2

AI doesn't replace your judgment. It guesses at it. What has to stay yours, and where AI can take you further.

By Mario Suarez · November 24, 2026 · 31 min read

This is an opinion piece. The sourced news brief for that week is Issue 023: AI found the holes. The rush to fix them took systems down..

Also published on LinkedIn, where you can join the discussion.

2,400 years ago, a friend of Socrates asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The oracle said no one was. Socrates couldn't believe it. So he went around Athens and questioned politicians, poets, and craftsmen. He found wisdom in them. He also found they claimed to know things they didn't. And he realized his own wisdom came from something else: he knew what he didn't know. We remember it as "I know that I know nothing."

AI is a lot like the experts Socrates questioned. It has a wealth of knowledge, and it sounds just as sure whether it knows or not. So his question comes back to us. What do we actually know, and what's our part? Much of judgment comes from knowing what we don't, and asking before we assume. I'd argue that kind of questioning is distinctly human.

Part 1 was practical: how one person gets good at working with AI. Know It, Learn It, and Manage It the way you'd manage a capable new colleague. It ended on a harder job: deciding which answers I really need.

Part 2 asks a broader question. When we hand work over, to a person or to AI, what's ours and what isn't? AI will keep improving, but I suspect our part stays critical.

That part is ours to own. It isn't just how we get better work out of AI. It's part of what makes us distinct, and part of our dignity. And it rests on a central human condition: judgment.

Answers got cheap. Spending our judgment didn't.

Before AI, research, analysis, and expertise could be hard to find, slow to get, and expensive to keep. Now answers are nearly free, and so is producing almost anything. AI's answers are confident, plausible, and fast, and it's easy to be a little enamored with that. So we give AI too much, and trust it too much, without seeing its gaps in judgment.

Here's how three handoffs compare. With the right judgment up front, we get more out of what comes back, and we can own it.

This isn't intuitive. You'd expect cheap production to make the work easier. Instead it runs into two things. Production outruns absorption: AI can produce more than any of us can take in, and all of it still needs us. And the Delegation Tax: the work it takes to own what comes back.

The upside went up. So did the Delegation Tax. Judgment is how you get the first without paying all of the second.

That's because managing AI is partly like managing a person and partly like running software, but it's different from both. It needs its own approach, in four ways:

  • Volume of what comes back. One colleague can only do so much in a week. AI can produce far more than I can take in. So a good handoff takes me much further, and a loose ask produces a lot of loose work.
  • Ownership of the work. A colleague shares the job of questioning the brief. They check it, think about the context, socialize it with others, and come back with questions if my ask is vague. AI is getting better at asking, but its questions come from a small slice of what a colleague would know. It rushes to resolve, and it can quietly fill in a missing decision and hand it back as finished work. All of that owning is on me.
  • New patterns of mistakes. People and software make mistakes too. But a person can usually tell you what they assumed, and software can be tested. AI's work looks finished whether it's right or not, and it can be right on one run and wrong on the next.
  • What I need to know. With a colleague, I mostly need to know the work. With AI, I also need to know AI: its personality, its traits, and its tools. That technique matters, but judgment matters just as much: using AI well, on the right things. Getting value takes both.

Quality is where the risk sits. AI infers your context, guessing what you need and filling in a lot of blanks. Some of the risk is in its answer, where it can still make things up. More is in your question, because AI infers that too.

So the bottleneck moved. It used to be getting the answer. Now it's knowing where to point all that capability, and which answers I actually need. Whether we mean to or not, everyone who uses AI becomes a manager. Judgment and management sit at the core.

Four capacities of human-centered judgment

Judgment is a broad term, and AI uses it too. It judges how to solve a math problem, which restaurant to recommend, and how to help with almost anything we ask. So the useful question isn't whether AI has judgment. It's which part of judgment is ours.

In Part 1, we outlined three human conditions: the parts of the work that stay with people when AI can do the rest. The first is absorption, taking in and understanding what gets produced. The second is judgment, and it's what this part is about. The third is alignment, making a decision real with other people. It gets a part of its own, next.

Judgment is deciding what's right for this situation. It tells us what we own, what we choose, what we hand over, and what we own again when it comes back. Four capacities make it human: wisdom, nuance, taste, and creativity.

Why do these four stay human? I see three reasons.

The first is agency. It's anchored in free will, which is part of how many of us understand what it means to be a person. It's why we vote, and why we take part in markets. We should own our own destiny. Judgment is how we steer our own lives and shape the world toward what matters to us. It's also why we answer for our choices. We live with what follows. AI doesn't.

The second is human dignity, and our uniqueness. What matters is different for each of us. Our beliefs, our relationships, and our experiences come through in the calls we make. Even lunch shows it. Stand in line at Chipotle and watch how differently people build the same meal. That difference is part of what makes us special, and respecting it is part of human dignity.

The third is practical. AI infers context. It has read our philosophers, but it doesn't know our situation. It guesses at the situation, and then at the answer, usually in the same pass. So the misses land right on these four. It can miss the whole picture, which is wisdom. It can miss the subtle difference, which is nuance. It can't know what we prefer, so it reaches for the average, which is taste. And it draws from what was already there, which limits creativity. One study of about 2,200 college essays found that each new human essay added more new ideas to the pile than each new AI one.

I suspect these four capacities will prove durable. As AI advances, the practical reason may fade in places, because AI will get better at some of these calls. The first two reasons won't fade. They're at our core, and they're a big part of who we are. AI can simulate human judgment, because it has seen so much of it. But human judgment should remain ours to own. It's how we shape our world. My hope is that by naming the human conditions, and the role judgment plays, we become more human, not less.

Applying judgment to our work

So that's human judgment. Let's explore what it looks like applied to real work. We'll look at two dimensions:

  • The work in front of you: the answer, or the thing being made. What kind of judgment does it need?
  • The direction behind it: what we're trying to do, and whether anyone has decided. How settled is it?

They sound alike. They aren't. The four capacities apply to both.

Bounded or subjective

The first dimension of judgment is how contained the work is. Can it be defined within a set of parameters, so that different answers inside them would all do the job? Or does it call for more subjectivity, where someone has to weigh what matters?

My daughter once decided to sort a bookshelf in our house by color. We could call it a bounded job. She set the guardrails: this shelf, these books, sorted by color. Another person doing the work would likely reach similar enough results, and they would match what she set out to do. The shelf still had plenty of variables. The books came in different sizes, and the colors didn't fall into a clean gradient, so she made judgment calls along the way. Some moves would have broken the guardrails: sorting by the Dewey Decimal System, throwing books out, or moving the shelf to another room. Inside them, there was a lot of room for discretion, and the job still worked. And her plan had already decided more than it said.

So bounded work has to fit inside guardrails we know and can state. Work within them, and reasonable people reach outcomes that are similar enough, and that match what the person asking intended.

At the other end is subjective work. Instead of sorting the shelf, ask how to decorate the room. Or, while touring a house, ask whether we could picture making it work for us. Questions like these can eventually lead to a bounded one, like sorting a shelf. But for now the guardrails aren't clear, basic choices about the answer are still open, and reasonable efforts can end in real disagreement. That's where the work leans on wisdom, nuance, taste, and creativity.

Both ends of this spectrum can be simple or complex. Picking an ice cream flavor is small, and it still takes taste. A checkout for crafts you sell online, with discounts, shipping, and refunds under rules you've set, is big, and it's bounded. Deciding how your store will compete is subjective. Once you've made that call, even building the whole website can become bounded. So bounded doesn't mean easy, and subjective doesn't mean hard.

Subjective doesn't mean anything goes, either. Some answers are better than others, but no set of guardrails can pick the best one. Someone has to choose and stand behind it. Where a call sits goes back to the reasons from the last section: the more subjective it is, the more it's ours to own.

Often real work is a mix: bounded steps around a few subjective calls. Part of the skill is finding those calls before you hand the job over. Then name the guardrails, because AI does its best work inside them. Name them, and you can hand over more of the mission, not just the steps. Leave them unnamed, and it fills them in with its own guesses. Naming them is also how a lot of subjective work becomes bounded: someone states a preference, and it becomes a guardrail. Not all of it should. Creativity often lives in the choices nobody has fenced in yet.

Settled or open

The second dimension is how settled the direction is. Has anyone decided what we're trying to do, and why? Or are we still working out the question?

In high school, I fried chicken in a restaurant kitchen. It looked like a job with no decisions in it. It wasn't. We argued about every step: one piece at a time or a handful, how many in the fryer basket, when to shake it. Each one was a call between speed and quality. But a lot had been decided before I picked up a piece: what we served, and what kind of restaurant it was. Somebody else had settled those. What was open, for us in the kitchen, was how. In other words, the strategy was settled. The tactics were still open.

Sometimes the direction is settled: someone chose, on purpose, and we've agreed on what we're trying to do. Then the work is to produce. Fry the chicken. Sort the shelf by color.

Sometimes it's open: we're still working out the question, so the work is to explore, not to produce. What should this restaurant serve? What would make this room better? Open isn't a problem to fix. It's where better ideas come from.

But a lot of what we call settled has assumptions buried in it. That's a third case, and a different kind of problem: assumed. Everyone is acting as if it's settled, but nobody actually decided. It's the most dangerous place, because it looks like settled and behaves like open.

Here's the test: can I say who decided this, and why? If I can, it's settled. If the honest answer is "not yet," it's open. If it's "nobody, we just act as if someone did," it's assumed. Settled doesn't mean certain. It means someone chose, and could tell you what would make them choose again.

The two dimensions complement each other. Bounded or subjective is about the answer: can a set of parameters tell us a good result? Settled or open is about the question: did someone choose what we're doing? They move separately. We can agree to rearrange the living room for conversation and still have to choose a layout. That's settled, but subjective. Or you can sort a shelf perfectly before anyone asks whether the room needs it. That's bounded, but open.

This matters with AI because it tells you what to ask for. On open work, I want AI to widen first: more options, more angles, the tradeoffs. On settled work, I want it to finish. Mix those up and you get a finished answer to a question you hadn't finished asking. Here, wisdom asks what this is really for, and creativity asks what else it could be.

At either end, the answer may be not to use AI at all. Some open questions are simply ours to work out. And very settled work often runs better on a formula, a rule, or ordinary software, with AI building the tool instead of being it.

The Human/AI Judgment Matrix

Now put the two dimensions together. On each one, human-centered judgment is what pulls us toward the human end. Overlay them, and you get a matrix of where we should, or could, engage AI, and how. I call it the Human/AI Judgment Matrix.

The principle underneath it is that how we should decide depends on how sure we are about what will work, and how much we agree on what we want. Let's apply this to AI: what can (or should) I hand to AI, and what stays with me?

The matrix shows four zones: the human zone, then collaboration, then delegation, and in the corner, automation.

The matrix also shows where assumptions cause trouble.

  • Misdirected effort. Treat an open or assumed direction as settled, and you get a perfect answer to the wrong question.
  • Misplaced judgment. Hand subjective work over as if it were bounded, and AI makes a call that was yours. We choose the wrong market to enter. Or we approach a friend the wrong way, on advice from an AI that doesn't really know our situation or the other person. We didn't own what we should have.

Both sit in the bottom band of the matrix because they are often the consequence of misplaced assumptions, but they can happen anywhere on it.

The second misstep is harder to catch. Bounded work can be checked, so a mistake can show up. A subjective call can't be checked the same way, so AI's choice just looks like someone's judgment. If I had to name one risk on this matrix, it's that one.

The matrix creates a construct to help choose what to hand over and how to work with AI. How far we let AI run also depends on how it has done on this work before, what happens if it's wrong, and who has the authority to act. Those questions grow when the work crosses people and teams. That's alignment, and it's where Part 3 goes.

AI makes us all managers, and we will need to sharpen our management skills and learn what it means to work with a synthetic team. Most individual contributors have had no one to delegate to, so calls like these have mostly been a manager's. With AI, everyone makes them, every day. And cheaper production doesn't make a wrong aim cheaper. It makes it run faster, and the cost grows with every person it reaches. So these skills belong to everyone who uses AI, not just the people with "manager" in their title.

Reading the matrix: AI engagement strategies and failure points

Read the matrix by zone, and it shows four ways to engage with AI: keep it human, AI as a collaborator, AI as an operator (delegation), and AI as a builder (automation). The last two are easy to mix up: an operator keeps doing the work, while a builder can leave behind a tool that does it. Each strategy puts your judgment in a different place. Each one also has its own ways to go wrong, and they lean one of three ways: handing AI too much, keeping too much, or drifting over time.

Let's explore these through an AI project of mine: The Ashburn Pulse, a weekly newsletter about family events around Ashburn, VA. My wife, Tricia, and I run it with a newsroom of AI roles. I told how it started in my Citizen CEO piece. It's a small project, but that's what makes it a good example. It shows how quickly one piece of work crosses every zone. Bigger work adds more people, more at stake, and more systems, but the same dynamics sit inside it.

Keep it human

The first question we faced with The Pulse was open and subjective: should we start a newsletter at all? Underneath, it was a question of what to spend our time on. For me, a few reasons came together. Our family would benefit from having the area's family events in one place. I wanted to learn AI, and I learn it best through experience, so this was a great chance to do that. And on the small chance it hit a home run, and people used it, great. If not, it was still worth it.

Engagement strategy: keep it human. Some things are meant to be human.

In this zone, AI isn't part of the decision process. That's also where the zone ends. Once AI is putting options on the table, we're collaborating, even if the final choice is still ours.

On the matrix, its home is the upper right corner, and it runs along the whole outer edge, wherever the work is open or subjective. Work there benefits from human judgment: wisdom, nuance, taste, and creativity. And I can choose it anywhere on the matrix.

Why keep work or decisions human? I see four reasons.

  • To control our own destiny. We choose how we spend our time, and we explore what we like, instead of taking what's put in front of us.
  • To grow through each other. We're social beings. We're meant to learn and grow with each other, not each alone in our own chat with AI. Some things stay human because it matters more that we learn from each other.
  • To get to a better answer. AI pulls toward a finished answer, its taste is the average, and it's weak at nuance. Some problems are just better addressed by people.
  • To go faster. Just because you could use AI doesn't mean it helps. For a quick email, you can spend more time going back and forth with AI than you would writing it yourself. Know when it makes sense.

Some work stays human because it fits us best. Some stays human because we choose to keep it.

Two things can go wrong here, and they pull in opposite directions.

Failure point: letting AI choose for you. The answer will sound reasonable, which is the problem.

AI will slip into calls that should have stayed ours. At work, there was an effort with a list of improvement ideas, and I had to ask: did we make this list, or did AI make it? Either could be fine. But I didn't know whether we were still keeping it human, or whether we'd moved into collaboration without noticing. That matters. When people come up with ideas, they've applied their judgment to them. When AI does, we don't know. Either way, we still have to judge the quality. In this case, AI made the list. It had signs of AI writing, and some ideas were good, but it didn't have enough rigor.

With work from other people, we think about their likely biases, strengths, and gaps. We need to do the same with AI. And if the list came from AI, we still have to be the ones choosing the direction. A Stanford-led study of how people use chatbots for personal questions shows how easily it happens. People ranked "decide for me" fourth among their own uses. In their actual chats, it was first.

For leaders, can you tell which zone the team is really in? It's hard to see judgment, and it's easy to be enamored with AI's output. If what a team ships looks just like what AI sent back, week after week, either AI is always right or nobody is deciding. And while agreement isn't proof of a problem and edits aren't proof of judgment, be aware that AI's judgment is limited. Adept use of AI preserves judgment. A beginner's use is likely to overtrust AI. So enable your team's AI development and push for preservation of human judgment. It could be as simple as asking three things: what was the key choice, what else did you consider, and what would have changed your mind? A team that decided can answer.

Failure point: holding on. Check every line and rebuild every choice, and you become the slowest step in your own work.

Holding on to too much is the lesser mistake here. When the call is about purpose, erring toward human judgment isn't a bad place to be. But AI can do so much now that some of what we hold on to is really habit, and we call it judgment. If AI could show you more than you'd find on your own, consider letting it in. That's where collaboration starts.

AI as a collaborator

Once we decided to do the newsletter, we had to figure out how to run it, without ever having worked in a newsroom: which sources to watch, what to call it, what roles we'd need, and how to publish. None of those had a right answer yet.

So I worked through each one with AI, back and forth. I knew it needed a brand, so I asked for several to choose from. I needed sources, so I asked for a list of local ones. I needed a website and a mailer, so I asked how to set them up. AI put options on the table, and I chose. The choices stayed ours, but the work didn't have to be human-only. That's the move in this zone: take a subjective decision and break it into bounded pieces.

The weekly publishing run still works this way. The team brings a draft and alternatives, and we choose what runs. Once, it called for an active social engagement and marketing strategy. I said no. I didn't want to invest that much time.

Engagement strategy: AI as a collaborator. AI widens what we see. We decide.

Examples of how to turn a subjective question into bounded pieces:

  • Ask for options. Names, designs, or approaches to choose from.
  • Gather what's out there. Size a market, scan competitors, sort customer feedback.
  • Borrow perspectives. How would a CIO see this? A 24-year-old who shops at Whole Foods? These are echoes, not the real thing.
  • Test your instinct. Check a hunch built on experience, to expose blind spots and challenge assumptions.
  • Reopen old assumptions. Start with decisions settled by scarcity, like checking a sample because we couldn't check everything.

There's judgment inside each AI collaboration, though: what counts as the market, and who counts as a competitor. In my own field, I can make those calls well, and AI takes me much further. Outside it, the risk goes up.

Failure point: choosing outside your expertise. AI can lay out options in a field I don't know, and they'll all sound good. If I can't tell a strong option from a weak one, I'm not collaborating. I'm guessing, with better formatting. This can happen in every zone.

Collaboration also spins up the other AI engagement strategies. When you work this way, you may find yourself standing up micro-software or an on-demand team to help with the work, and then setting it down when you're done.

That's how AI lets us go further than before, and it's where the difference is. Every company can rent the same models and get the same average answer. What can't be rented is which questions we won't let it close, and which standards we won't let it average.

Failure point: the production trap. Once, I asked AI for a market analysis and got eleven areas, with plans inside the plans. I was still deciding whether to do it at all, and AI was already planning how. The work comes back faster than you can think, and it's a relief to have it. Relief is a bad reviewer.

More options don't add up to a better decision. Across 106 experiments, people working with AI beat people alone, but on average lost to whichever was better on its own, mostly on decisions. A larger 2026 preprint from the same team found the same pattern. So ask: does the answer have a job? If I can't say what I'll do with it, I skip the handoff.

Failure point: skills fade. Skip the long way, and you lose the feel for when an answer is off.

Forming my own view takes twenty minutes. Asking takes four seconds, so I ask first. That's hardest on people starting out. We built our judgment on plain work that got corrected while little was at stake, and that's the work AI now takes. In 1983, Lisanne Bainbridge called this an irony of automation: machines take the routine work, and people meet the hard cases with skills they no longer practice.

So build the practice with intent. Before a newer person sees any AI drafts, have them write their own answer and the reason for it. Then have them find the assumption buried in three drafts, and say which, if any, changed their view. We used to learn judgment by doing the routine work. We may have to learn it by taking the machine's work apart.

The same goes for the rest of us: move human judgment to the front. Make your own call first, then look at what AI says. Going first isn't enough by itself, though. In a study of college students, those who answered first still took 62% of ChatGPT's wrong advice. Adding a few reflection questions cut that to 40%. So before I hand over anything that matters, I write three lines: what I think, what I'll do with the answer, and what would change my mind.

AI as an operator

Once we set up the newsletter's approach, I handed AI a bigger job: put the newsroom together. The first newsletter came back fast, and its draft wasn't very good. I didn't know why it was picking what it did.

Engagement strategy: AI as an operator. AI runs the work and makes calls along the way.

A single agent can operate a process. My way of doing it is a synthetic team: AI roles working together through a process, with people responsible for the result. Give each member a role, define how they work together, and put human roles in it too, not just a person at the end. My newsletters run this way now, with AI roles for research, writing, editing, and checks, and human roles for review and approval. Like software, a team needs architecture: people, process, and data.

Ask for too much at once, and AI takes it and runs. Specify everything up front, and the spec becomes as much work as the job. What works is closer to bringing a person onto a team, and that's where AI has an edge I'll come back to: the Iteration Advantage.

If you're doing something for yourself, give the agents more room. If you need a process that repeats, build a team. Either way, organizing the work can move to AI. Answering for it can't.

Failure point: deferring. A team that shows up that fast looks done, and it's tempting to take what it hands you.

That's AI's default pull, the red arrow on the matrix: it is remarkably effective at turning half-formed questions into finished answers. I call that trait the Resolver, and it pulls hard, even on experts. In a study Anthropic published in October, professional analysts followed an AI agent's quietly set lean 86% of the time, on choices where either answer was defensible. Having a person in the loop isn't the same as having a person's judgment in the loop.

Failure point: running outside your expertise. AI will do almost anything you ask, and an operator keeps doing it, run after run. That doesn't mean I can judge what comes back, or manage the team doing it.

Expertise includes management. A synthetic team is still a team, with roles, handoffs, and reviews. When the work goes wrong, the fix is often in how the team is set up: a missing role, a handoff that drops context, a review in the wrong place. If you don't know how to design and run teams, you'll keep correcting answers instead of fixing the team. With people, we rarely hand a job to someone when we can't tell good work from bad. With AI, the handoff is so easy that we skip that step. This is Socrates again: know your limits. That doesn't mean you can't use AI there. It means knowing the tradeoffs you're making.

AI as a builder

The newsletter needed a home and distribution, so I asked AI to build a website and the tools behind it: a database of sources and a publishing system. Once Tricia and I approve an issue, posting it should happen the same way every time. That part is settled, so it doesn't need AI's judgment each week. AI's best job there was building the tool, not being the tool.

Settled work is a candidate for both building and operating. If the steps can be written as rules, build a software tool. If it needs human-like judgment and reasoning, establish a synthetic team.

Engagement strategy: AI as a builder. AI builds working technology, and it runs on rules we chose.

I think we're going to see a lot more people building small tools for their own work. Let's call it micro-software: a small tool built for one need, by the person who has it. Plenty of processes never made sense to automate. They were too small, too messy, or changed too often. On the other end, some software is built for thousands of different customers, making it overbuilt and sometimes awkward for each user.

AI changes that math. It can empower people to build micro-software that's more effective because it's built for the specific process need. We don't have to write the rules ahead of the work anymore. They can come out of it, one correction at a time. Once they're written, the result runs the same way every time, and people who aren't engineers can now build it.

Today, most company data lives inside the software that uses it. I suspect we'll see enterprise data separate further from enterprise software, to unlock micro-software and synthetic teams.

The automation corner of the matrix fits work that is bounded and settled, though micro-software can change rapidly as the work does. The four capacities don't disappear there. They go into the rules: wisdom about what to check, and taste about what good looks like. AI helps build the machine. The machine runs on rules we chose.

I think AI as a builder of micro-software will become as common as AI as an operator, or more common. We picture big systems that need a business case. The significant opportunity is smaller and more common: the people who own a piece of work learning to build for it. That's the Citizen CEO, as an AI builder and an AI operator. As we invest in the tools and support that let people build like this, we will continue to be amazed at what we make.

Failure point: too rigid. Push enough work into rules and standards, and you lose the complexity that was in it. The customer is usually the first thing to go.

Rules age, too. Yesterday's taste, written into a rule, can become tomorrow's assumed. When people run a process, they question it for free. A machine runs the old answer perfectly, long after the people who knew why have moved on. We automated yesterday's process and then wondered why tomorrow didn't show up.

So the smart move is to use AI to become more flexible, not less. Harden what has settled. Leave the rest alive. Moving work into the corner doesn't end the need for judgment. It moves it, from checking every item to checking the rules. Every rule we hand to a machine still needs an owner, its reason written down, and a date to ask whether it's still right. And some steps should stay human on purpose, like approvals and permissions, for security and accountability.

The Iteration Advantage

One way to read the matrix is by zone. Another is to watch the line between them. Call it the horizon: where we've set how we use AI today. That line can move, and faster than ever. That may be the bigger change AI brings.

The reason is how we shape the work. When we bring a person onto a team, we don't hand them a full spec of everything they'll do. We set the mission, hand over a first piece, correct what comes back, and hand over a little more. The role takes shape as we learn. Software has worked the other way: write the requirements up front, then release it all at once. AI can be shaped like a person, at the speed of software. As a builder, it can change a tool quickly. As an operator, a team can add a skill or a source in a sentence. I call this the Iteration Advantage: how quickly the work can evolve. For some work, we no longer have to hire new people, or wait to build, buy, or partner for software.

The Pulse showed me how it works. I didn't know the requirements when I built the team. I wanted good links in each issue. First, AI gave me one that didn't work. Then it linked to an aggregator instead of the source. Then to the source, but not the page itself. Each correction became a rule the team now checks on every issue: use the primary source, link to the exact page, make sure it opens. I'd never have written those down on my own. I didn't know I knew them. A parent who clicks should land on the event, not have to go find it. AI should make things easier, better, and more personal. The parts that were taste stayed with me. A check can remember my rules. It can't tell me what matters.

A correction that only fixes today's answer is a patch. A correction that changes the next run is a capability. That's how the newsroom grew: AI let me, usually in minutes, add beat writers, an eats feature, a guide to annual events, and wider sources. We review every issue before it runs, so we see right away whether a change took. The scope of AI's purpose grows as we learn.

The horizon lets us dynamically learn. When changing a system was expensive, we settled more before we began, and we lived longer with what we chose. Now we can build a tool for work that isn't fully settled, and change it or throw it out when our needs change. And work that hardened years ago can be opened up again. We need work that is dependable while it runs and changeable as we learn.

There are two ways to waste the advantage. The first is churn. Fix answers week after week without building the team, and you'll pay the Delegation Tax over and over. Correct a person, and they adjust their own process. AI often won't. It forgets, because it doesn't carry that context forward. A synthetic team can be remarkably effective and flexible, but only if someone keeps its process current, so it evolves instead of drifting. That takes knowing how to break work into people, process, and data. Without that, the investment doesn't pay back, and you're really still using AI as a collaborator.

The second is hardening. Automation pulls toward settled, as the builder section showed. Things harden that should stay open, and that has a cost. Our checks passed every week and still couldn't see the venue that had stopped posting, or the event listed only on a paper flyer. One week, Tricia noticed a fireworks show on Facebook, and we realized we'd missed it. The expensive failure is the one that passes every test you knew how to write.

So who should watch the line? The people closest to the work. For a long time, they couldn't build a team or change their own tools, so we hardened our processes around our technology. The software became the process. That may not have to be true anymore. And someone still has to own the call, including the call to move work back.

Putting the four together

Most real work uses more than one strategy. Once I had the site's infrastructure set up, which needed my permissions, I handed it back to AI. The team has kept improving it since, as operator and builder together, and I've stayed out of the technical details. Then I adapted the newsroom for a second newsletter, AI Above the Cut.

At each turn, I chose what to hand over, how far, and when to take it back. That's what managers do. Judgment is management. One person who owns a piece of work can now staff it, with AI and people, and manage it end to end.

What I've found is that engaging AI well takes the same skills as managing a team. You set the direction. You scope the work. You put each piece in the right hands, human or AI. And you set it up so the pieces come together. On the bounded side, that looks like testing: decide what good looks like, write it down, and check the work against it. At the horizon, it looks like what good managers have always done: brief well, coach, review, and be clear about who makes which call.

As we expand our use of AI, I suspect we'll find much of the work sits on this dynamic horizon, and successful AI engagement will look a lot like human management.

And management nests. Every strategy has tactics, and every tactic has a strategy. An executive's open question, like what kind of restaurant to be, sets the direction. Inside it, each person runs a piece, with open questions of their own, like how to fry the chicken. So each of us becomes an operator inside someone else's direction, and AI is useful at every level.

The bookshelf shows it too. "Sort the bookshelf by color" sounds settled. But look at what that plan already decided: that the shelf should look good, that sorting by color is the way to do it, that the shelf stays where it is, and that every book stays. None of that was said. All of it was treated as chosen. Every plan, like every ask, settles the bigger questions above it and leaves open the ones inside it. Sometimes sorting the shelf really is the job. But it's worth asking: have I actually chosen this direction, or am I carrying it in from habit?

While AI can produce in abundance, the scarcity of time and judgment doesn't go away. It moves. My real limit isn't how much AI can make. It's how much I can absorb, manage, and decide in a day.

Judgment, the human condition

We all can fall into the trap of trusting AI too much with the arrival of each confident, plausible answer. On the other hand, we can feel intimidated, even threatened, because it can seem too human, and it sometimes challenges our livelihoods.

Using AI has also made me appreciate what makes being human special: things like wisdom, nuance, taste, and creativity. That's the list I'm starting with, and it might not be the right one. But these are things that help us celebrate our lives, and explore who we are, individually and with each other.

As we work with AI and expand its use personally and professionally, I believe it's critical for us to understand human-centered judgment. Come back to the three reasons judgment stays human. There is a real, practical benefit today: human judgment is what makes management work, and AI, in a synthetic space, is best managed by people. But the other two are what I hope we walk away with. Agency: our ability to author our own work, and to help shape the world around us. Human dignity, and our uniqueness: the calls only each of us can make. That's our role.

This is an edition that I will dog-ear and come back to from time to time. Every generation has had to work out what a new tool means in its life. We're working out our relationship with AI in real time. These are our human conditions, and they're worth understanding, embracing, and fighting for.

If you've read other editions, you'll know I refer to the two bridges we need to cross as we adopt AI: Capability to Value, and Value to Human Good. Our judgment is at the center of both journeys.

The next question for us to discuss is what happens around us. My work gets better before our work gets different. That's the third human condition, alignment: how we decide differently, together. It's where Part 3 picks up.
