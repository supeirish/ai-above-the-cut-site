The 15 minutes before you post

The least glamorous task on the list is often the highest-leverage. Be useful in the conversation before asking the conversation to notice you.

Spend 15 minutes before and 20 minutes after each main post.
Leave five real comments: two interpreters, two operators, and one source cited this week.
Choose posts from the last 2–4 hours with a real argument and room for a useful response.
Write 40–80 words: name their point, add the fact only you have, and leave a narrow opening.
Never lead with your newsletter link. The comment earns the profile click; the profile does the selling.

Post copy

OpenAI runs a test that counts how often a model will answer requests to break into systems.

Its flagship model answered 1.5% of them.

Then they turned off the safety layer sitting in front of it — the filter, not the training. The kind of switch people imagine unleashes something.

It went to 2.0%.

Reaching 95% took something else entirely: training a separate model for the job.

So the scarce thing was never the permission. It was a model somebody built on purpose and then decided who could use.

And that is the part worth an executive's attention. The gate on powerful AI did not move from governments to vendors. It split in two. Governments still set the legal boundaries. OpenAI now runs a second gate inside them, deciding who qualifies and on what terms.

Vendors have always sold in tiers. What is new is that the tier decides how the model behaves on safety, not just what you pay.

You are not buying a model. You are buying an access arrangement.

Three questions to put to your vendor are in this week's brief.

(One caveat we put in the issue and will repeat here: 95% measures willingness to answer, not whether an attack works. On OpenAI's own evaluations the general model still performs better at standard-setting exploit development and at writing vulnerability reports.)

Thread beats:

1/ OpenAI turned off the cyber safety layer on its best model.

Completion of advanced hacking requests went from 1.5% to 2.0%.

Reaching 95% took a purpose-trained model.

The scarce thing was never the permission. 🧵

2/ The test counts how often a model will answer requests to break into systems. Chaining flaws, getting past a login, taking more access than an account should have.

It measures whether the model responds. Not whether the attack works. Hold onto that.

3/ GPT-5.6 Sol, the flagship: 1.5%.

Same model, guardrails removed: 2.0%.

That is the switch everyone imagines is the dangerous one. It moved the number by half a point.

4/ GPT-5.6-Cyber, trained for the job: 95.0%.

Two caveats OpenAI states itself. The previous cyber model was already at 57.3%, so the real jump is 57.3 to 95. And OpenAI rates the new one High, same as the general model, below its Critical threshold.

5/ Here is the part I would not skip.

95% is willingness to answer. On OpenAI's own benchmarks the general model still performs best at standard-setting exploit development, and better at writing vulnerability reports.

Mixed, not a clean capability leap.

6/ So what changed for a buyer?

The gate did not move from governments to vendors. It split in two.

Governments still set legal and export limits. A second gate now runs inside them: who qualifies, through which channel, under what controls.

7/ Vendors have always sold in tiers.

What is new is that the tier decides how the model behaves on safety — not just price and support.

8/ Which means the thing you are buying is an access arrangement, not a model.

Three questions for any vendor bringing you AI capability are in the brief.

We lowered a grade on our own scorecard this week.

We had Bank of America at Stage 2 on the Margin-Proof Tracker — the level that requires a financial number actually tied to AI.

Re-reading the filing, it does not clear that bar. The efficiency ratio is there. The AI adoption numbers are there. Nothing in the document connects them.

Notes for whoever posts this

No imageNo follow-up comment
Post copy

- Do not say OpenAI "unlocked" a hacking model or "removed the guardrails and it went wild."
The entire point is the opposite: removing the guardrail did almost nothing. Overstating it
destroys the one insight the post carries.
- Keep the mixed-benchmark caveat. On OpenAI's own evaluations the general model still wins on
two of the four named tests. Leaving that out would make the post a stronger claim than the
evidence supports, and this publication's whole position is that it does not do that.
- Never write "95% success rate." It is a completion rate — how often the model answers. Not
whether an attack works. That distinction is the post.
- The Tracker post is deliberately unglamorous and slightly self-damaging. That is the point. It is
for the readers deciding whether to trust the publication, not the ones who share it.
