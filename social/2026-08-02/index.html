The 15 minutes before you post

The least glamorous task on the list is often the highest-leverage. Be useful in the conversation before asking the conversation to notice you.

Spend 15 minutes before and 20 minutes after each main post.
Leave five real comments: two interpreters, two operators, and one source cited this week.
Choose posts from the last 2–4 hours with a real argument and room for a useful response.
Write 40–80 words: name their point, add the fact only you have, and leave a narrow opening.
Never lead with your newsletter link. The comment earns the profile click; the profile does the selling.

Post copy

Visa cut roughly 2,600 roles last week. It also beat estimates, grew revenue 14%, and raised guidance.

That combination is the story, not the number.

The cuts landed in product and development — the function that builds what Visa sells. On the earnings call the CEO said teams that used to be ten or more are being reorganised into "agentic squads of two to four," and that for teams using the agentic toolchain there are 80% more code commits and feature development is more than 65% faster.

Then he was asked where the savings go. The list ran long: acceptance, cross-border, risk and security, marketing services, Pismo, Featurespace, B2B, Visa Direct. And then — stablecoins, agentic commerce, Visa as a Service.

Two of those are mechanisms by which a payment could one day settle without touching a card network at all.

So a company beating and raising shrank the function that builds its product, and pointed part of the proceeds at its own disintermediation risk.

One thing Visa never did: say how much of the reduction AI actually caused. No percentage. Not once.

The question I'd ask of any AI-enabled cut on your own plan: what is the freed budget pointed at, who owns it, and by when? A reduction that funds only margin is an efficiency case. One that funds a named bet is an option.

Wednesday — the second cut (LinkedIn)

No imageNo follow-up comment
Post copy

Two AI labs disclosed last week that their own safety testing reached real companies' production systems.

Anthropic reviewed 141,006 evaluation runs and found three incidents where third-party evaluation containment failed. In one, a model published a booby-trapped package to the live PyPI registry. It ran on 15 real systems.

The detail that should matter to you: of the organisations Anthropic reached, none had detected the activity themselves.

None of them had a relationship with Anthropic. They were not customers. They were not in a pilot. They were on the internet.

Anthropic is explicit that the model did not deliberately escape — it calls this "closer to a harness and operational failure than a model alignment failure," which is a meaningfully different thing from OpenAI's separately disclosed zero-day escape.

Your third-party AI risk register probably lists your model vendors. It almost certainly does not list your model vendor's evaluation vendor.

That is the gap this week exposed.

Event — Primary source: both labs published first-party incident reports.

If you own vendor risk: does your assessment reach past the vendor to the parties they test with?
