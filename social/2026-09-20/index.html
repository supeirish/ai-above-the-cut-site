The 15 minutes before you post

The least glamorous task on the list is often the highest-leverage. Be useful in the conversation before asking the conversation to notice you.

Spend 15 minutes before and 20 minutes after each main post.
Leave five real comments: two interpreters, two operators, and one source cited this week.
Choose posts from the last 2–4 hours with a real argument and room for a useful response.
Write 40–80 words: name their point, add the fact only you have, and leave a narrow opening.
Never lead with your newsletter link. The comment earns the profile click; the profile does the selling.

1 · LinkedIn - primary, post Sunday evening

Post copy

Microsoft licensed one AI tool to more than 200,000 of its own employees.

By its own account, that changed nothing.

The company published five lessons from its AI transformation this week. Lesson one is that
it first treated AI like any other rollout: deploy the tools, provide training, drive
adoption. In its own words, a tool "licensed to over 200,000 people does not change how the
work gets done."

What changed things was redesigning the work. After that, the sales organisation tripled
adoption of priority use cases. The cloud supply chain team mapped and simplified its
workflows first, then deployed more than 100 purpose-built agents, and reports cycle time
down by up to 75% in selected workflows.

These are company-reported numbers from selected teams, not audited results.

The useful part is the order. Access first did nothing. Redesign first did something.

If you are reporting a seat count as progress, you are reporting the thing Microsoft just
told you does not work on its own.

Full issue: https://aiabovethecut.com/2026-09-20

2 · LinkedIn - the unit post, Tuesday or Wednesday

No imageNo follow-up comment
Post copy

Three numbers from this week, and only one of them is a unit you can manage.

A seat count measures access. Microsoft just showed what access on its own is worth.

A token bill measures consumption. GitHub's engineering team put about $120,000 of token
spend against porting the Copilot runtime to Rust, plus about three weeks of a developer's
time. Other team contributions are not costed anywhere in the write-up.

Neither number tells you what one finished, reviewed piece of work cost.

DoorDash published something closer. About $4.79 and 13.8 minutes per stale feature flag,
with usable pull requests for 45 of 50, against its own estimate of one to two hours by
hand. That figure excludes engineer review at two points, and it covers 50 flags out of more
than a thousand.

Even with those caveats it is a better number than a seat count, because it has a
denominator: one finished unit of work.

Cost per finished, reviewed unit carries the review time, the rework, and the cases a human
took back. Ask for it. If nobody can produce it, that is the finding.

https://aiabovethecut.com/2026-09-20

3 · X / Twitter - thread, Sunday night

No imageNo follow-up comment
Post copy

1/ A three-month trial gave 133 patent lawyers an AI drafting assistant. Then it took the
tool away.

The seniors were better without it than lawyers who never had it. The juniors, on average,
were not.

2/ While the assistant was in use, drafting quality rose 0.34 standard deviations at ten
days and 0.38 at ninety, with the bigger gains among junior lawyers.

So during use, juniors gained more.

3/ In the final exercise, 91 of the 133 reviewed and redlined an existing AI-generated
application with no AI help.

Lawyers who had used the assistant scored 0.32 standard deviations higher than those who
never had it.

4/ That gain was not spread evenly. The authors: "this advantage was concentrated entirely
among senior lawyers (0.45 SD, p = 0.02). Junior lawyers showed no average gain."

5/ Here is the part most threads will miss. The juniors did not simply fail.

Their scores split. Fewer middling results, more of both the poor and the good.

"No average gain" is hiding two groups.

6/ Limits, because they matter: a working paper, one profession, three months. A third of
the sample never finished. And the authors ran no baseline test of unaided skill before the
trial began.

7/ The read: the seniors kept an edge with no AI in front of them, which the authors take as
learning their existing expertise made possible.

Whether that holds outside patent drafting is untested.

https://www.nber.org/papers/w35720

6 · Reply-guy preparation

No imageNo follow-up comment
Post copy

"This is just Microsoft marketing." The post is marketing. The admission inside it is
still an admission, and it is against the seller's interest to say a tool licensed to
200,000 people did not change the work. We label it company-reported, from selected teams,
with no independent validation. Say that first and the objection loses its edge.

"The lawyer study proves AI deskills juniors." It does not. It shows no average gain for
juniors and a split in their scores. There was no baseline test of unaided skill before the
trial, so nobody can say anyone was deskilled relative to where they started.

"$4.79 is obviously fake." It is DoorDash's own figure, reported through a trade outlet,
and it excludes engineer review at two points. We say all three things in the issue. The
number is useful as a benchmark to test against, not as a promise.

"So AI isn't delivering." Not our claim. Four items this week show gains that required
investment around the model rather than the model alone: redesigned work, training, a
platform layer, and labelled data. The argument is about what you have to buy, not about
whether anything works.
