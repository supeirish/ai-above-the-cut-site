The 15 minutes before you post

The least glamorous task on the list is often the highest-leverage. Be useful in the conversation before asking the conversation to notice you.

Spend 15 minutes before and 20 minutes after each main post.
Leave five real comments: two interpreters, two operators, and one source cited this week.
Choose posts from the last 2–4 hours with a real argument and room for a useful response.
Write 40–80 words: name their point, add the fact only you have, and leave a narrow opening.
Never lead with your newsletter link. The comment earns the profile click; the profile does the selling.

1 · LinkedIn - primary, post Sunday evening

No imageNo follow-up comment
Post copy

Anthropic searched 481 million of its own transcripts last week.

That search did not find the incident it was looking for. Something else did, weeks earlier,
while staff were assembling transcripts to share with an outside evaluator.

The 481 million was the second pass. It was a check for anything worse. It found nothing worse.

Here is why that matters more than the number.

The records existed the whole time. Every transcript of every action. The problem was never
that the evidence was missing. The problem was turning it into an answer to a specific
question, and the first attempt, across 141,000 transcripts, missed.

Most enterprises are one incident away from learning the same thing about their own agents.

One exercise, this week. Pick one completed agent task that used an external tool. Ask its
owner to show you the original instruction, the systems it reached, what it changed, and who
approved it. Ask them to export what your team can retrieve on its own.

You will learn one of two things. Either you have a gap, or you have the capability and have
never used it. Both are worth knowing before you widen an agent's authority.

2 · LinkedIn - the Corrections post, Tuesday or Wednesday

No imageNo follow-up comment
Post copy

This week we re-opened all fifteen rows of our Margin-Proof Tracker at their original sources.

Four were wrong.

One of them, a JPMorgan row, rested on a link that no longer resolves. The filing behind it
does not support the claim we published. It mentions AI once, as a macro tailwind, and
attributes expense growth to headcount going up, not down.

We printed all four corrections with the corrected fact and a working link.

The issue they appear in argues that a clean public record is a weak signal, because the
record alone cannot tell you whether someone searched and found nothing or never searched.

That applies to us. So here is our answer: we searched, we found four, and they are printed.

Ask your vendors the same question. Not "have you had an incident." Ask how they look, what
the last search found, and what changed afterwards.
