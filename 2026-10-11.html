01 · The One Thing
Google wants one AI agent to handle work across a company's tools, picking the AI model for each job. Google introduced the Gemini agent on October 8 as "your new single, universal agent for work."

Staff give it a goal, and it returns finished work. Google says a task can run for days after the laptop closes, and the agent remembers the project. Per job, it picks Google's Gemini or Anthropic's Claude. A company can set a hard cap on a project's AI spending, which Google says the agent enforces. No price or outside test yet. CIO Dive reports a preview for select customers. (Google · CIO Dive)

  The executive shift: the agent, not the model, is becoming what a company buys. Google says the agent picks a model per job "to deliver optimal quality and lower your costs." That could push model makers to compete on price inside Google's product. What stays put is what the agent learns about your company. A Futurum analyst told CIO Dive that is "where the real switching costs will sit."
02Do This Week
1 · Set the spending cap and exit terms before staff use a work agent. Owner: your chief information officer. Our read: switch on a hard spending cap where the vendor offers one, as Google now does. Ask whether what the agent learns about your company can be exported, and whether its work runs on the vendor's servers. Stakes: the agent's knowledge of your work is the part that is hard to move later.
2 · Time how long code waits for review before adding coding agents. Owner: your head of engineering. Our read: compare six months before and after each rollout: time from submitted to approved, the share sent back, and work items closed. Stakes: in the study in The Synthesis, code and review time rose, but finished work did not measurably rise. A firm may pay for the tool and again in review time.
3 · Name who signs off before an AI tool acts in your company's name. Owner: your general counsel. Our read: pick someone who did not build the tool, and ask the vendor in writing what it covers if its AI causes harm. Stakes: Washington wants AI companies to report and fix incidents. The UK regulator says an agent's autonomy is no excuse for poor compliance, and Singapore says financial firms stay accountable for the AI they use, including a supplier's (The Signal).
03The Signal

This week's question: as the agent becomes the way work gets done, who answers for what it does, and who gets paid?

Trust  Who answers when an AI agent makes a mistake? Governments are starting to say: the company that built it or runs it. Reported: Axios, October 9. Primary: Anthropic, October 9; UK and Singapore regulators, October 8 and 7. Anthropic disclosed that its AI models filed forms on real sites, mostly during tests, including a false police tip. The White House's new Super Intelligence Force then said its process for disclosing and fixing incidents is "not optional." Axios said the statement did not make clear what enforcement or penalties would look like. The UK data regulator said an agent's autonomy "is not an excuse for poor compliance." Singapore's financial regulator says financial firms "remain accountable" for AI in their services, including a supplier's, from October 2027. Our read: the pressure to report and fix AI mistakes is arriving before the rules that define it. (Axios · Anthropic · ICO · MAS)
Deploy  GitHub let companies bill AI code review to the organization, and said its usage reports missed some agent work. Primary: GitHub, October 6 and 8. Organizations can now bill Copilot code reviews to the organization, paid from AI Credits, with an optional budget. By default, a review "fails" once an engineer's quota runs out. GitHub also said its usage reports left out most agent activity from some code-editor versions: "We can't backfill missing data." Analysis: review is where one study found coding gains stall (The Synthesis). A return case built on that dashboard may have a gap. (GitHub, Oct 8 · GitHub, Oct 6)
Horizon  OpenAI's yearly sales pace is near $50 billion, not the $70 billion widely cited, and chip stocks fell on the news. Reported: FT and Bloomberg, October 8. The FT said the higher figure came from investors trying to compare OpenAI with Anthropic, which counts sales made through cloud partners; OpenAI does not. Bloomberg reported, citing people familiar, that OpenAI expects to reach $70 billion or more in annualized revenue by year-end. Our read: much of the AI buildout is financed against a few labs' revenue claims, and the labs do not count revenue the same way. Ask how any figure you rely on was counted. (FT · TechCrunch)

05The Synthesis
Count what ships, not what the tool writes

The best evidence this week on whether AI coding tools pay off is not new. A Harvard working paper by Fiona Chen and James Stratton, last revised August 4, was reported by Ars Technica on October 9. It used work records from Jellyfish, which sells engineering analytics, for about 726,000 workers at 718 firms, from 2021 to March 2026.

After firms adopted AI coding agents:

Code went up. Pull requests, the batches of code sent for checking, rose 23% per worker.
Checking slowed. Review of each one took 49% longer, from an average of about seven days. The share sent back nearly doubled.
Finished work did not measurably rise. Completed work items rose by too little to rule out chance.
Jobs did not measurably change. The authors "cannot attribute significant employment changes to AI."

They point to a "bottleneck from the code review process." A National Bureau of Economic Research paper on GitHub and app-store data finds the same shape.

Analysis: on this evidence, time saved writing code is largely absorbed by checking it. The buyer has not yet been shown to keep a gain, while the seller is paid by seat or token either way.

The counter-case. It is a working paper, not peer reviewed, and its data end before the newest agents. Jellyfish supplied the data, reviewed the paper only for confidential information, and sells this kind of measure. Work items are a rough stand-in for finished software. (Chen and Stratton · NBER w35275)

The finished-work test

Our read: before renewing or expanding an AI tool, get three numbers.

What got finished, before and after? Work items closed, not code or drafts produced.
How long does the check take? Time waiting for review, and the share sent back.
What does one finished task cost? Not the price per token or per seat.

If the first number has not moved, you are probably paying for activity, not output.

What We're Watching

Dated tests, each with the source for its date.

Can a business run its AI model on its own computers? · by Saturday, October 31. Mistral says it will release Mistral Large 4's weights, the trained model itself, "by the end of the month," and that the model "will be able to run on private cloud or on-premise." It has not named the license. Reflection says Beam follows "later this month" under Apache 2.0, which allows commercial use. Our read: running the model yourself means paying for computing power, not per answer. The next test: whether both ship, and on what terms. (Mistral · Reflection)
Anthropic's new limits on how Claude may be used · Thursday, November 12. Anthropic's updated usage policy takes effect. It bars using Claude to track people without consent, to "decide or recommend who to investigate, arrest, or charge," or to build surveillance tools. Its weapons section now says the ban includes "the software and components that make weapons work." Injury-capable equipment Claude controls needs "a qualified operator" able to stop it. Our read: a model maker's usage rules become your rules once your staff and agents use its model. The next test: whether Anthropic says how it will check. (Anthropic, Oct 8)
Will businesses adopt a standard for customer AI agents? · within a month of October 9. Sierra and Meta proposed the Personal Agent Protocol on October 6. Sierra published a draft, called Poppy, on October 9, and added 35 design partners, including Bank of America, Visa, Mastercard, Target and OpenAI. Customers decide what access their agents get; each business decides what agents may touch. The next test: the reference implementation Sierra says it will publish over the next month, and whether businesses let customer agents write, not just read. (Sierra, Oct 9)
Two comment windows close. Thursday, October 15, 11:59pm: NIST's draft guide on AI for cybersecurity work (Issue 014). Tuesday, October 20: the commodity regulator's request on futures tied to computing power, with 38 comments as of October 9. (NIST · Federal Register)
