01 · The One Thing
AI found the holes. The rush to fix them took systems down.

Outages hit Microsoft, the FAA, JPMorgan, United Airlines, Apple and the National Security Agency over eight days. On November 18, officials told Congress these were not attacks. The FBI saw "no indication whatsoever" of malicious activity. At least some outages came from rushing to patch AI-found flaws, CIO Dive reported. The NSA's network went down while patching a flaw in PHP, a web language, the national intelligence director said. Microsoft blames its own November 12 to 13 outage on a network upgrade, not AI. (CIO Dive · Banking Dive · CFO Dive)

  The executive shift: The cost of AI-found flaws now includes downtime, not only break-ins. Citi's chief executive, Jane Fraser, said the patching now going through the world's technology at the same time is "five-, tenfold what we've ever seen before." It "shouldn't be a complete surprise to anyone that that doesn't always go well," she added (Banking Dive).
  Justin Herring, a partner at the law firm Mayer Brown, put the gap plainly: "The capacity to find vulnerabilities has grown much faster than the capacity to fix them." Banks in Project Glasswing, Anthropic's cyber defense program, "have been absolutely inundated with questions from their regulators," he said.

04Do This Week
1 · The outages were not attacks, officials said, and at least some came from patching AI-found flaws, CIO Dive reported. Ask your most critical vendors how they test urgent patches, and how much warning you get before planned downtime. Owner: your chief information officer, with vendor management. Our read: a vendor's patch can be your outage (see 01 · The One Thing).
2 · Citi told customers this week that they carry the loss when a third-party AI agent with account access goes wrong, even if the agent is hacked. If AI agents act for your customers on your systems, or for your staff on other companies' systems, write down who carries the loss. Owner: your general counsel, with fraud and digital teams. Our read: PNC and Citizens took similar steps, and JPMorgan and Bank of America did so last month, TechCrunch reported. Whether customers accept this is untested. (TechCrunch)
3 · Microsoft's Autopilot agent, billed by use with no price yet, began rolling out to some customers. Before any always-on agent goes live, set a usage budget and name one person who watches the bill. Owner: your chief information officer, with finance. Our read: an agent that works without waiting for a prompt also runs up usage without one (see 03 · Product).
05The Signal
Proof  Anthropic's record IPO priced above its range. Trading since has been bumpy. Primary: Anthropic. Reported: The Register, Information Age, TechCrunch; Reuters headline. Anthropic priced its shares at $83 on November 18, above the $70 to $78 range we watched for last week. Shares opened at $111.50 on November 19, up 34%, The Register reported. They closed the day at $97.17, up 17%, Information Age reported. The shares fell 13% the day after their debut, Reuters reported. Anthropic has also made $900 billion in spending commitments, mostly for computing, TechCrunch reported, citing a filing. Some of those agreements can be ended early. Our read: demand to buy in was strong. What the company has committed to spend is far larger than what it raised. (Anthropic · The Register · Information Age · TechCrunch · Reuters)
Horizon  Nvidia's sales doubled. It says it will be choosier about funding its customers. Primary: Nvidia. Reported: Axios, TechCrunch, The Verge. Nvidia reported quarterly revenue of $115.8 billion on November 17, up 103% from a year ago. It expects about $130 billion next quarter, plus or minus 3%, with no China data center sales assumed. Interim CEO Colette Kress said Nvidia will be "disciplined and selective" in backing neoclouds, the newer cloud firms that rent out AI computing. She also said its loan guarantees take effect only once a customer has its own financing. She called that a clarification of existing terms, not a new policy, TechCrunch reported. The board does not intend to bring back Jensen Huang. The shares still fell 46% over seven straight weeks, The Verge reported. Reuters reported that SEC staff are looking at potential accounting issues. Our read: last week's test was what the interim team would say about financing. Its answer was more selectivity, and the first public account of when its loan guarantees apply. Strong sales did not stop the slide. (Nvidia · Axios · TechCrunch · The Verge)
Deploy  KPMG ran an audit with more than 100 AI agents. Reported: Financial Times (via The Irish Times). KPMG UK ran the agents on the financial statements of an unnamed FTSE 250 company, alongside its normal audit. In several cases "the AI was able to go so much deeper," and asked "better questions," said Cath Burnet, its UK head of audit. In one area, a human had to step in. A team built the agents in four months. KPMG did not rely on the AI audit, and plans to expand the project to up to 20 larger audits. The UK audit regulator warned that AI could pose a "distinct threat" to auditors' independence if they use what the agents find to advise their audit clients. Our read: KPMG tested a different split of the work between agents and auditors. It is still one firm's test, on an unnamed client, and KPMG is the only source. (The Irish Times)
Trust  Attackers are using AI on the same patches. Reported: The New York Times, SiliconAngle, The Register, TechCrunch. On November 17, the startup Calif said OpenAI's GPT-6 Cyber helped it find a critical flaw in Java. About 90% of Fortune 500 companies use Java, The New York Times reported. Oracle released a patch, and the US cyber agency told organizations to "immediately patch." Google's threat team saw attackers use AI, with no human in the loop, to turn new patches into working attacks. It called this a first, SiliconAngle reported. The FBI and AWS, Amazon's cloud arm, warned that attackers are using an open-source AI hacking tool against critical sectors. AWS saw it used to steal API keys for AI services at "industrial scale," The Register reported. And Nadella warned that "there is a lot of stuff in our critical infrastructure that's unpatchable." Our read: a patch now starts a race. Defenders rush to install it, and attackers use AI to study it. (The New York Times · SiliconAngle · The Register · TechCrunch)
▼ Below the Cut
AI is wiping out entry-level jobs, the story goes. The newest data is less tidy. Primary: ADP Research. In October, employment for workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed jobs was 4.2% lower than a year earlier, per a tracker from ADP and Stanford. But the same young workers also lost ground in the least-exposed jobs, down 1.3%. Across all ages, the most-exposed jobs shrank 0.8%, while the least-exposed grew 0.3%. Our read: young workers are losing ground across the board, and more so in AI-exposed jobs. The data does not show how much of that is AI. (ADP Research)

07The Synthesis
Finding got cheap. Fixing did not.

This week, AI showed how quickly it can surface software flaws and how deeply it can probe professional work. Fixing, checking and showing a return are still harder.

Software. AI models are surfacing flaws faster than teams can safely patch them. "The capacity to find vulnerabilities has grown much faster than the capacity to fix them," Mayer Brown's Herring said. At least some of the recent outages came from that gap (see 01 · The One Thing).
Work. KPMG's agents dug deeper in parts of an audit. But a human still had to step in, and KPMG did not rely on the result. Few companies have "clean" enough data for agents to work with, KPMG's Burnet said (see 05 · The Signal).
Money. 64% of insurance CEOs plan to put 11% to 20% of their budget into AI, but 1% report a measurable return (see 02 · Three Numbers). Nvidia, which has helped finance the build-out, now says it will be more selective about that help.

Analysis: the edge may go to organizations that can absorb change quickly, not only to those that adopt AI first. AI now helps banks, auditors and others find far more weak points. Fixing them still takes testing, downtime and people who can check the machine's work. This week, that is where the strain showed.

The counter-case. This may be a one-time catch-up. Many of these flaws have always existed, TechCrunch notes; AI is just finding them fast. Fraser expects more outages for a while, then says the patching "will make us all a lot more resilient." Dave DeWalt, a former McAfee chief executive, called the outages "growing pains of a security system that's getting stronger, not weaker." If they are right, the strain fades as the backlog clears. (Banking Dive · TechCrunch)

08Where the Minds Disagree
 
Is the AI money real, or circular?

The case that it is real. Nvidia's quarterly revenue rose 103% to $115.8 billion. Anthropic took in $17 billion of revenue in the third quarter, TechCrunch reported. "The revenue is all coming from other customers," Joey Brookhart of SemiAnalysis told TechCrunch. TechCrunch adds that with the IPO, the system "might just become self-sustaining," as public investors replace suppliers in paying the bills. (TechCrunch)

The case that it is circular. Amazon and Google are Anthropic's investors and also its biggest suppliers. The money they put in comes back when Anthropic buys their computing, which TechCrunch called "the same money passing from one pocket to another." Separately, seven suppliers bought $26.0 billion of Anthropic's shares in the private sale, and Anthropic has deals to buy from all but one of them. Nvidia says its loan guarantees apply only once a customer has its own financing. The S&P 500 is down 10% from its October high amid AI worries, AP reported. (TechCrunch · AP)

Our read: both can be true. The revenue is real money, but part of it starts with the sellers. What would settle it is spending by customers who are not also suppliers, and who report a return. In insurance, only 1% of CEOs report one so far.
