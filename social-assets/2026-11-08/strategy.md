## 1 · LinkedIn newsletter companion (Sunday night)

**Headline:** Who gets to build with AI? Goldman just drew a line.

**Subtitle:** What stood out to me this week, and the full list of headlines.

*AI Above the Cut is my free weekly newsletter on the AI news that matters for business leaders. Subscribe at aiabovethecut.com/subscribe.*

This is my quick editorial on what rose above the cut this week. One story stood out. Goldman Sachs took AI coding tools away from most of its bankers and traders, Bloomberg reported on Monday, November 2.

My take in one line: **Restricting a tool and restricting who can invent better ways of working are different decisions.**

Three parts:

1. **What Goldman did.** Coding tools now go to engineers.
2. **Where I land.** Goldman may be right on risk. The test is the path from idea to working tool.
3. **Three questions for leaders.** Who builds, what can they change, and who answers for it.

At the end, a quick inventory of everything in this week's issue.

[Heading] What Goldman did

Under guidance updated in October, Claude Code and GitHub's coding tools are now for engineers, Bloomberg reported. Quant staff known as strats are exempt. Goldman still runs its own in-house AI assistant.

Traders had used the tools to track client flows. In July, Goldman's technology chief praised this kind of "vibe coding" by non-technical staff. Goldman also described a model where staff build the first version and engineers harden it. ([Bloomberg via The Edge](https://theedgemalaysia.com/node/824273))

The timing is worth noting. Companies are spending far more on AI, not less. Menlo Ventures puts yearly AI spending at $177 billion, an annualized run-rate estimate as of September, up from $33 billion in 2025. AI coding alone is about $34 billion of that, mostly Claude Code and Codex. It is a survey-based estimate, and Menlo invests in Anthropic. ([Menlo Ventures](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2026-the-state-of-enterprise-ai/))

So spending is rising, and at least one big bank is limiting who gets to use the tools.

[Heading] Where I land

Here is my read. Goldman may be right on risk. Powerful coding agents carry real risk, and code built by non-engineers still needs review.

But a tool limit and a builder limit are not the same thing. The people closest to the work are often the ones who find new ways to do it. Goldman's traders were taking a first step toward what I have called the **Citizen CEO** ([essay](https://aiabovethecut.com/editorials/ai-and-the-citizen-ceo.html) · [on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/ai-citizen-ceo-mario-suarez-1qoke)). That is a person who can build a capability on demand and keep shaping it, around work they understand.

That happens in what I call a **Synthetic Space** ([essay](https://aiabovethecut.com/editorials/work-was-built-for-two-kinds-of-things.html) · [on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/work-built-two-kinds-things-ai-third-mario-suarez-7eyxe)). It is a place where people and AI build together, led by teams across the business, close to the work.

I think the test is not "tool or no tool." **The test is whether the people closest to the work still have a governed path from idea to working tool.** The rules belong in the work, not in a queue. Where the stakes are high, add formal sign-off.

Closing the door slows the learning. Guardrails, review and a path to production keep it going. That is also what I mean by **Better for More**: use AI to make work better, then extend that improvement to more people. A narrow door does the opposite. (From my [Synthetic Space essay](https://aiabovethecut.com/editorials/work-was-built-for-two-kinds-of-things.html).)

[Heading] The fair counter-case

One bank is one case. Goldman keeps its strats on the tools and runs its own assistant, so this is not a ban on AI. A narrower start may be a sensible first step, and I do not know what Goldman will do next. What I would watch is whether the path back to building stays open for the people who found the uses.

[Heading] Three questions for leaders

• Who in our business can build with AI, what are they allowed to change, and who answers for the result?
• When someone builds something useful, what is the path to production, and how long does it take?
• Do our rules live in the work, or in a queue?

The question I'm left with: **if you limit the tool, who keeps the job of inventing the better way?**
