## 1 · LinkedIn — primary, post Sunday evening

OpenAI says GPT-6 Astra "saturates ARC-AGI-3 with a 99.9% score."

ARC Prize runs that test. It published two results at that same reasoning setting.

99.9% on OpenAI's own setup, where the model keeps its private working state between turns.

54.8% on ARC Prize's shared setup, which every model gets and which makes the model write down what it wants to remember.

Same model. Same reasoning setting. 45 points.

The wrapper of prompts, tools and memory a model is run inside is called a harness. OpenAI's footnote does name the harness it used.

The 54.8% is nowhere in the announcement.

Both numbers are records, and ARC Prize calls Astra a real step forward. This is not an accusation, and ARC Prize is not making one. It says outright that these are two different questions.

But they are also two different products.

One measures a model. The other measures a model plus the vendor's plumbing.

And the gap is wider still further down the table. At low reasoning effort it is 17.5% against 98.0%.

**Here is why that matters on Monday.**

The same week, Anthropic shipped Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 as the same underlying model, at the same published base price, separated by safeguards and by who is allowed the less restricted one.

So in one week: a score turned out to depend on the test setup, and what a product could actually do turned out to depend on the guardrails.

**The model is no longer the whole product.**

The system around it — memory, tools, permissions, safeguards — is becoming part of the capability. Your supplier builds that system.

Which means "which model?" is on its way to being the wrong procurement question.

The better one: who ran the test, what setup was the model given, and was the rival it beat tested the same way?

Ask for it in writing.

## 2 · LinkedIn — credibility post, Tuesday or Wednesday

Our reviewer read this week's issue and told us our own lead was the weakest example in it.

She was right, and the finding is better than the headline.

Our argument was that control over the conditions a model is measured and sold under is becoming a real source of supplier power.

Three things this week fit that. But they are not equal, and the test is simple: **can a buyer get it another way?**

**Weakest: the benchmark gap.** Yes, ARC Prize published 54.8% and 99.9% for the same model at the same reasoning setting. But ARC Prize closed that gap inside a week by committing to publish both conditions, clearly labelled, from now on.

An advantage a third party removes in seven days is not supplier power. It is an information asymmetry that closed.

**Middle: OpenAI's own framework.** Astra was graded the first model at the "Critical" cybersecurity level, against a framework OpenAI wrote, with OpenAI deciding the threshold was crossed.

Outside evaluators did test Astra — its system card carries external cyber evaluations by Irregular, plus work from UK AISI and Apollo Research. So the honest claim is narrower: outside testing constrains the claim. It does not constrain the framework the claim is made under.

**Strongest: Anthropic's Mythos.** Same underlying model as Fable. Same published base price. Fewer safeguards.

You cannot buy it. You apply, and Anthropic decides.

That is the one to watch, because it is the only one of the three that no third party can close and no budget can solve.

We led with the weakest of them. We are saying so, because the ranking is the useful part.

## 4 · X — short thread, the cost inversion

1/ Buried in ARC Prize's Astra results is a number that breaks a common budgeting assumption.

2/ On the shared harness, maximum reasoning effort cost $26,098. Reasoning off cost $49,791.

3/ More thinking was cheaper. ARC Prize explains why: the model solves games in fewer moves, so there are fewer calls.

4/ This is one interactive benchmark. It does not establish a general rule.

5/ But it does kill "more reasoning means more cost" as a safe assumption. On agentic work, more thinking can cut the number of actions enough to lower the bill. Test it on your own workload before you cap effort to save money.

## 6 · Friday split — the narrow disagreement

Is AI still driving layoffs?

Challenger's August report is the one source that puts a number on AI as a stated reason. AI was cited in 3,462 August cuts. That is the fourth-most cited reason, its lowest monthly total since December 2025, and it ends a five-month run as the leading monthly reason.

Year to date, AI has been cited in 116,175 announced cuts, about 22% of all cuts.

Both numbers are true and they point in opposite directions.

The figure that will get quoted is the year-to-date 22%. The figure that actually moved is the monthly one.

Our read: a single month is not a trend, and we are not calling one. But if you are budgeting off "AI is the top reason for layoffs," that stopped being true in August, and it stopped quietly.
