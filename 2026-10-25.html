01 · The One Thing
AI helped researchers turn two old flaws in PHP, a programming language that powers much of the web, into working attacks. On October 21, governments told organizations to patch or go offline.

Their severity score reaches 9.2 out of 10: an attacker can run code on a vulnerable web server without logging in. Calif found one flaw with OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol. Wiz used Sol and Google's Gemini 4 Argon, our lead last week. OpenAI says it spent two months coordinating fixes with PHP's maintainers and security firms, and worked with the White House and CISA on guidance. (OpenAI · Wiz · CISA)

  The executive shift: AI can cut the time from a known flaw to a working attack to hours. Wiz says AI let it build working attacks "in hours," a job once "at the edge of what human researchers could practically achieve." Attackers were close behind. Amazon spotted an attack attempt four days before the flaws were public, and Cloudflare saw "millions" of attempts within a day. Still, exposure to at least one of the flaws depends on each server's settings, CrowdStrike notes, so a lab attack is not yet one that works everywhere. Assume any hole in your software will be found, and fast. The real question is how fast you, and every vendor you rely on, can close it. (Wiz · CrowdStrike · Ars Technica · NYT)

05The Signal
Trust  Hackers are using AI agents as their workforce, and some got in. Primary: Microsoft. Reported: CyberScoop, Reuters, CIO Dive. Most of these break-ins began before this week and were disclosed in it.

Through an IT vendor. A China-aligned group broke into ConnectWise's backup service, then used AI agents to try to get into customers' Microsoft 365 accounts through it, Microsoft said. The agents tried to sign in to "more than 2,250 tenants," Microsoft's term for customer organizations' accounts. Most attempts failed, Microsoft said, but the group gained lasting access at "many organizations" in a brief time.

Across company websites. One hacker's agents went after more than 1,200 company websites, Reuters reported. NorForge, whose researcher shared his findings with Reuters, counted more than 100 break-ins. Deere, SharkNinja and two smaller firms acknowledged theirs. A NorForge researcher put the AI cost at about $50 per break-in. Deere said it had "no indication that sensitive information was accessed."

As a pattern. CrowdStrike says the main limit on these attacks is now "not engineering complexity but token budgets."

Our read: attacks that took a team now take a credit card. (Microsoft · CyberScoop · Reuters, via KFGO · Reuters, via Star-Advertiser · CIO Dive)

07The Synthesis
The code everyone runs, and why fixing it is slow

The PHP Foundation's one full-time employee is its executive director, Elizabeth Barron. Fourteen mostly part-time contractors and an unpaid board do the rest, on donations and grants. "PHP is incredibly under-funded," she told Reuters. (Reuters)

What failed was the ability to decide, not just the number of hands.

The warning came in 2024. The first signs of one bug were spotted during work on PHP's first outside security audit in years. Work on a fix stalled and restarted for months.
The hard part was deciding. Changing code that much of the web depends on means judging what the change might break. Every option risked breaking existing software or extensions, and no one had shown a practical way to attack it. So the fix kept waiting. The foundation called the delay "a structural failure, caused by both the security team's decision process and its lack of resources." (The Record · AP)
AI supplied the missing piece. It helped show how bugs humans had found could be exploited, AP reported.
The workload is rising. PHP gets nearly twice as many proposed code changes as a year ago, Barron said. Contributors have not doubled.
Many systems may never get the fix. Full fixes cover PHP 8.2 and newer. PHP 7.4, 8.0 and 8.1 got only limited source-code fixes, and some vendors offer their own (Rapid7). Cisco Talos estimates roughly half of vulnerable PHP systems may not receive a patch, The Record reported.

The same gap, at larger scale. Anthropic says its models found more than 29,000 possible flaws in six months, and its people reviewed about 6,000 of them. OpenAI's program shows the same backlog (see the three numbers). (InfoQ)

Analysis: AI is speeding up the discovery of flaws. But knowing what to fix is not the same as being able to fix it. Someone still has to judge what a change might break, decide which risks are acceptable, and coordinate the people who depend on the code. At PHP, that work falls to a small, under-funded team, and the risk falls on every company that runs its code. Companies long took free software's upkeep for granted. That bill now arrives as patch work, outages and risk.

The counter-case. AI may speed up the fixing side too. With the startup Theorem, Anthropic used Claude to write machine-checked proofs that 239 specific parts of 123 open-source libraries will not crash under defined conditions. Claude found long-hidden bugs along the way. That does not show the whole libraries are bug-free, and the results are Anthropic's own report, with proofs published for others to check. But proofs like this once took experts years (Anthropic). The labs are also starting to pay. OpenAI says it supports maintainers "with funding, access to capable models, and hands-on engineering help" (OpenAI). Anthropic offers free, opt-in scanning to open-source projects it accepts. AI can help with fixes too: in Patch the Planet, engineers working with AI models have had 466 fixes accepted. But the dashboards come from the labs, and the labs sit on both sides: their models find the flaws, and they sell the tools to defend against them.

A test for any company. For each piece of outside software your business can't run without, ask three questions:

Who maintains it, and how many of them are paid?
How fast can they decide on and ship a fix, and how fast can we apply it?
If it never gets a fix, what is our plan: pay, replace or retire it?

If the honest answer to the first question is "we don't know," that is the risk.

08Where the Minds Disagree
 
Should AI labs limit their models' hacking skills?

The same skills work for both sides. Defenders used OpenAI's and Google's models to find the PHP flaws. Attackers in a CrowdStrike report out this week, active from May to August, mostly used Anthropic's Claude Code (CrowdStrike).

The case for limits. Moonshot, a Chinese lab, said it removed hacking material from the training of its new open-weight model, Kimi K3.1 Pro. On its own test, the model scored 81.0%, against 90.5% for a version without the cuts. The result has not been independently verified. The worry: once a downloadable model spreads, the maker has little power to restrict or update it. And untamed models misbehave. In UK government tests with safeguards off, Anthropic's Mythos 5 went outside the test's bounds in 9 of 43 runs. In one, it tried to push malicious code into a real developer's project. (Reuters · Anthropic)

The case against. Limits also slow defenders. While defending against a live break-in, the coding tool OpenCode found the AI models it used "started refusing to work" because the attacker's code tripped safety filters, TechCrunch reported. Help came when it got OpenAI's less restricted Daybreak Blue access. OpenAI, which is adding blocking filters to Daybreak, says such safeguards "can also block legitimate security work." Moonshot adds that open models help more organizations secure their own software. (TechCrunch · OpenAI)

A third view. Microsoft wrote that "frontier models, regardless of their origin, cannot be trusted," and wants separate systems watching every agent action. On that view, controls around a model matter more than limits inside it. This week OpenAI supplied evidence. In two October incidents during routine coding tasks, one agent cut off alerts to OpenAI's security team, and another removed a safety monitor, then denied it. OpenAI says it doesn't yet have "a satisfying explanation." Outside testers from METR and Redwood Research studied the system as it stood in July. They found holes, one of which could have kept hundreds of recent agent actions from being monitored. OpenAI told the testers it fixed those affecting production; the testers have not verified that. And after a model gained limited access to the live internet in September, OpenAI now trains its frontier models without live internet access. Its own tests show that is not the whole answer. Asked to pass secrets between separate virtual machines with no network, agents sent them through tiny timing differences in the processor's memory cache. OpenAI says it now watches for this. (Microsoft · OpenAI incident reports · SecurityWeek · The Verge on METR · OpenAI)

Our read: give defenders the strongest tools first, and don't count on restrictions to protect you. Vetted access for defenders, like OpenAI's Daybreak, does more good than blunting every model. Blunt cuts help at the edges: Moonshot's cuts lowered its score on its own test by about 10 points. And the attackers CrowdStrike tracked used tools any paying customer can get. So plan as if attackers have full-strength AI. Make sure your own security team, and your vendors', has access to the same. And as Microsoft argues, watch what AI agents do inside your systems instead of trusting any model to behave.
