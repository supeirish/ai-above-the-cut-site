01 · The One Thing
Goldman Sachs is narrowing where AI is allowed to play: it took AI coding tools away from most of its bankers and traders. Under guidance updated in October, Claude Code and GitHub's coding tools are now for engineers, Bloomberg reported on November 2.

Quant staff known as strats are exempt, and Goldman still runs its own in-house AI assistant. Goldman said it weighed "productivity and risk management." Traders had used the tools to track client flows. In July, Goldman's technology chief praised this "vibe coding" by non-technical staff. Meanwhile, company spending on AI is still climbing fast (see 02 · Three Numbers). The question is moving from who can use AI to who can build with it. (Bloomberg via The Edge)

  The executive shift: Restricting a tool and restricting who can invent better ways of working are different decisions. Goldman may be right to rein in powerful coding agents. They run on staff computers, which Replit's chief executive called a growing security risk this week, and code built by non-engineers still needs review. But the people closest to the work are often the ones who find new uses. Goldman's traders were taking a first step toward what we have called the Citizen CEO (essay · on LinkedIn): a person who can build a capability on demand and keep shaping it, around work they understand. That happens in a Synthetic Space (essay · on LinkedIn): a place where people and AI build together, led by teams across the business, close to the work. Goldman described one promising model in July: staff build the first version, and engineers harden it. The test is whether the people closest to the work still have a governed path from idea to working tool. The rules belong in the work, not a queue, with formal sign-off where the stakes require it. Closing the door slows the learning. Guardrails, review and a path to production keep it going. (The New Stack)
  Also this week: AI's build-out is running into three limits. Local measures to limit data centers passed in 44 of 45 completed counts, by Redbud Consulting's count. The S&P 500 had its worst day since April 2025, led by AI data center stocks. And companies are putting AI on a budget (see 05 · The Signal).
02Three Numbers
$177 billionMenlo Ventures' estimate of companies' yearly spending to deploy AI, as of September, up from $33 billion in 2025.
Why it matters: AI coding alone is about $34 billion, up 7.7 times, mostly Claude Code and Codex, the kind of tools Goldman just limited. Companies are spending far more on AI, and some are controlling it more tightly. It is an estimate built on a survey of about 400 US buyers, and Menlo invests in Anthropic. (Menlo Ventures)
58%The share of Anthropic's third-quarter revenue spent on compute, up from 48% in the second quarter, its filing shows.
Why it matters: Anthropic paid extra for short-term capacity, its filing says. Capacity is expensive for a supplier that needs it now, even as the price per AI task keeps falling. (Anthropic S-1/A)
42.8%The share of test runs that AI shopping agents failed in Visa's new benchmark of 22 models from 12 labs.
Why it matters: in 54% of the failed runs, the agent still chose to buy, even though it had missed some of the customer's needs or picked the wrong product. These were simulated decisions, not completed purchases. The cost of AI includes its mistakes. (Visa)

04Do This Week
1 · Before you limit who can build with AI, give the people already using it a supervised path to production. Owner: your chief information officer, with business leaders. Our read: a named reviewer, a test environment and clear rules for data let staff keep building while engineers check the work. Stakes: Goldman pulled AI coding tools from most bankers and traders (see 01 · The One Thing).
2 · Ask your two biggest AI vendors where your 2027 capacity will come from, and what your contract says if a site is delayed. Owner: your chief information officer, with procurement. Our read: for critical workloads, a written guarantee of capacity, service levels and the right to move work elsewhere may be worth more this year than a bigger discount. Stakes: new data centers now face local votes and slower approvals (see 05 · The Signal).
3 · Measure how many days it takes to install a security fix after it ships, for every system that faces the internet. Owner: your chief information security officer. Our read: AI tools can now find flaws by the hundred, so fixing time matters more than finding time. Stakes: in its own test, one OpenAI run produced 948 working Linux exploits (see 05 · The Signal).
05The Signal
Horizon  AI's build-out is running into voters and investors. Reported: AP, Bloomberg, CNBC, TechCrunch, InfoQ. Primary: OpenAI, Google. On November 3, local ballot measures to limit data centers passed in 44 of 45 completed counts, by Redbud Consulting's count, AP reported. On November 5, the S&P 500 had its worst day since April 2025, led by AI data center stocks, AP reported. The Nasdaq 100 lost a record $2.3 trillion, Bloomberg reported. One investor called a $1.5 trillion value for Anthropic's IPO "a stretch," CNBC reported. OpenAI has raised $11 billion so far at $1.25 trillion, TechCrunch reported, citing Bloomberg. The round could reach about $20 billion, short of the $30 billion OpenAI reportedly sought in September. OpenAI promised to pay the full cost of power at campuses it owns, and Google said the same for a new $17 billion site. Capacity is tight, but not everywhere. AWS says its large committed capacity is largely sold out through 2027, InfoQ reported. Microsoft is turning off computing it rented from Oracle, Bloomberg reported. Our read: investors are questioning what the build-out is worth. If that makes money harder to raise, some of the cost may reach buyers. (AP · AP on stocks · Bloomberg · CNBC · TechCrunch · OpenAI · Google · InfoQ · Bloomberg on Oracle)
Proof  Anthropic's sales and adjusted profit kept climbing. Its margin did not. Primary: Anthropic S-1/A. Revenue was $17.0 billion in the third quarter, up about 47% from the second. It reported a $567 million operating loss, after a $974 million operating profit in the second quarter. The filing gives two main reasons: $5.0 billion of pricier short-term compute, and $2.0 billion of non-cash charges tied to its employee donation matching program. On its own adjusted measure, operating income rose to $2.5 billion from $2.2 billion, while the adjusted margin fell to 15% from 19%. Its commitments and leases total about $895.4 billion, about half due after 2030, and about $213.8 billion of that can be ended or is conditional. The filing warns: "We do not expect our revenue will continue to grow at comparable rates." The price range is still blank, and we found no public reply to Senator Warren. Our read: demand held up. The margin is what slipped. Its path back depends partly on cheaper long-term compute, which may now face harder local approvals. (Anthropic S-1/A · The Register)
Deploy  Companies are putting AI on a budget. Reported: TechCrunch, The Register. Primary: Gartner. Goldman is limiting who gets AI coding tools (see 01 · The One Thing). Apple lowered some engineers' daily AI cap from $300 to $200 in October and plans cuts to as little as $100, Bloomberg reported via TechCrunch. Revolut cut its AI coding inference costs about 90% per token in under a year by running open models itself, its infrastructure lead said. Business AI token spend on Ramp grew just 1.5% across August and September, then picked up 8.9% from September 23 to October 23, TechCrunch reported. Ramp's customers are mostly venture-backed tech companies. Gartner analysts say open models can already handle many routine agent steps, and they project that by 2030, 90% of enterprise operational agent steps will run on them. Today the saving per task is "closer to five or six times," they wrote. And just 4% of customer service leaders have the return they expected from AI, Gartner found. Our read: the bill is now the metric. Cost per finished task matters more than how much AI people use. (TechCrunch on Apple · TechCrunch on Ramp · The Register on Revolut · The Register on Gartner · Gartner)
Trust  In OpenAI's own tests, AI now finds software flaws by the hundred. Vendor claim: OpenAI. Reported: TechCrunch, TechNode, VentureBeat. In one controlled run, OpenAI's pipeline produced 948 working Linux exploits, OpenAI says. Each could let someone who already has a low-level account on a Linux machine take full control of it. The run cost $1.2 million in API fees. Its new GPT-6 Cyber model found 81 previously unknown serious Firefox flaws, and Mozilla has fixed many of them. Censys found more than 800 open copies of Artex, a Chinese AI hacking tool, reachable online without a password, TechCrunch reported. Moonshot released the Kimi K3.1 Pro weights (the trained model itself) from our last issue, TechNode reported. Moonshot says it cut the model's hacking skills, VentureBeat reported; we found no outside test of that yet. Our read: AI is making the search for flaws far more scalable. How fast you patch is now the gap that counts. (OpenAI · OpenAI on GPT-6 Cyber · TechCrunch · TechNode · VentureBeat)
▼ Below the Cut
AI is replacing service staff, the story goes. Most service teams Gartner surveyed have not cut anyone for it. Primary: Gartner. Reported: The New York Times. Only 21% of 275 service and support leaders said they had cut agent headcount because of AI, Gartner found. Those that did cut about 16.7% over 18 months, and 7% of the leaders surveyed have already rehired. Gartner calls it the "cut fast, rehire later" trap. Microsoft expects fewer than 5% of the 60,000 offered buyouts to take one, and says the buyouts are not about AI, the Times reported. Our read: in the service teams Gartner surveyed, AI has not yet replaced staff on a broad scale, and some cuts are already being reversed. (Gartner · The New York Times)

07The Synthesis
The squeeze in the middle

Three different costs shape AI's economics, and they don't always move together.

Capacity got dearer. Anthropic's compute bill rose to 58% of revenue as it paid extra for short-term capacity (see 05 · The Signal).
Some AI tasks are getting cheaper. Open models can handle many routine agent steps for far less, Gartner analysts say, and Duolingo credits lower AI costs per feature for a higher margin (see 06 · The Margin-Proof Tracker).
Finished work is the cost buyers control. It includes errors, review, security checks and rework, not just model calls. Goldman weighed productivity against risk before narrowing who can use AI coding tools (see 01 · The One Thing), and Visa's test agents often chose the wrong purchase (see 02 · Three Numbers).

That third cost is where Goldman's decision sits. The best AI setup is not necessarily the cheapest model or the tool employees prefer. It is the one that reliably produces approved work at a total cost the company can accept, and someone has to own that result.

Analysis: a supplier can pay more for capacity and charge less per task, while its customer still cuts the cost of finished work. Tight capacity gives sellers some pricing power. Open models and routing give buyers a way out. Hard reasoning stays with the top labs. Routine steps go to the cheapest model that works.

The counter-case. Demand is still strong. Anthropic's revenue grew about 47% in one quarter, and ChatGPT added 200 million weekly users in two months. Analysts cited by Ars Technica expect the new measures to mostly hit projects planned for 2028, not those under construction or approved through 2027. Builders can also move: Google just committed $17 billion to a new site in Wyoming. The squeeze may prove short. (Ars Technica · Google)

A test for any AI plan this quarter. Ask three questions:

Which of our AI uses need a top model, and which could run on a cheaper one?
Who in our business can build with AI, what are they allowed to change, and who answers for the result?
If our main vendor raised prices 20% or slowed service, what would we do?

If the answer to the third question is "nothing," your vendor sets your AI budget.

08Where the Minds Disagree
 
Did voters reject AI, or just the data center next door?

The case that voters rejected AI. "What we just witnessed was an AI earthquake," Brad Carson of Americans for Responsible Innovation told Axios. Alejandra Montoya-Boyer of The Leadership Conference said the backlash was a call to regulate AI, not only a fight over utility costs. She cited NBC exit polls: more than half of voters called AI and data centers a crucial or major factor in their vote for Congress. Meta's president said the "underlying bigger issue" is "how people feel about AI in general." (Ars Technica · TechCrunch)

The case that it was local costs and politics. "Let's not confuse an anti-Trump wave with an anti-tech mandate," said Adam Kovacevich of the Chamber of Progress, a tech industry group. Voters ranked cost of living and corruption above AI and data centers, the same exit polls showed. About three-quarters of voters oppose a new data center in their own community, AP's voter poll found. Only about a quarter called data centers crucial to their vote. Concrete local impacts like power bills were "far more salient" than existential fears about AI, Blue Rose Research's director of research told Time, Ars reported. (Ars Technica · AP)

Our read: the vote was about the bill and the neighborhood more than the technology. That is why the industry's answer is to pay for power, publish water use and drop secrecy deals. If those deals work, building resumes at a higher price. If they do not, the next fight is over AI itself, in Congress.
